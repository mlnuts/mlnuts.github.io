    Section 1.10 Chi Square and Student t Distributions

    Chi square (\(\chi^2\)) and Student \(t\) distibutions are essential for statistical inference, i.e., interpreting and making sense of the data. Previously we have talked about Confidence Interval based on Central Limit Theorem where we need to deal with the data-provided estimates of the mean and the standard deviation. Chi square and t distributions augment that work and show us how to implement the inference more soundly.

    Subsection 1.10.1 Chi Sqaure Distribition

    Suppose we have \(n\) identical amd independent standard normal random variables. Chi square tells us about the probability distribution of the sum of the squares of these \(n\) variables.
    To write the PDF of the sum of the squares variable, let us introduce this variable as \(X\) and the independent standard normal variables as \(Z_1, Z_2, \cdots, Z_n\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} X = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 +\cdots + Z_n^2 \equiv \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2,\tag{1.10.1} \end{equation}
    where the PDF’s of all the \(Z_i\) variables are identical
    \begin{equation*} \rho_{Z_i}(z_i) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z_i^2/2},\quad (-\infty\lt z_i \lt \infty) \end{equation*}
    Then, by appropriately changing variables, we can show that the PDF of \(X\) is given by
    \begin{equation} \rho_X(x) = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}\;\Gamma(n/2) }\, x^{n/2-1}\,e^{-x/2},\quad (0\lt x \lt \infty,\ n \gt 0).\tag{1.10.2} \end{equation}
    We state this result more compactly as
    \begin{equation} X \sim \chi^2_n(x)\text{ or } \chi^2(x; n).\tag{1.10.3} \end{equation}
    Here \(n\) is said to be the degree of freedom of the \(\chi^2\) distribution. We now derive this result from first principles.

    Derivation.

    First, notice that the \(n\) variables can be thought as Cartesian axes in an \(n\)-dimensional real space \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\) With this perspective, \(X\) is just the square of the spherical radial coordinate in that space. We will exploit this view point below fully.
    The probability of an event for which the value of \(X\) variable is between \(x\) and \(x+dx\) will be
    \begin{equation} P( x \le X \le x + dx ) = \rho_X(x)\, dx.\tag{1.10.4} \end{equation}
    We can find the probability of this event from the joint probability of all the \(Z_i\text{'s}\) also as long as we restricted the \(Z_i\) values to satisfy Eq. (1.10.1).
    \begin{equation} P( x \le X \le x + dx ) = \left[\rho(z_1, z_2, \cdots, z_n)\, dz_1dz_2...dz_n \right]_{x = \sum_i z_i^2}.\tag{1.10.5} \end{equation}
    Now, since the \(Z_i\text{'s}\) are independent variables, joint probability factorizes.
    \begin{equation*} \rho(z_1, z_2, \cdots, z_n) = \rho(z_1)\rho(z_2)...\rho(z_n), \end{equation*}
    where the PDF’s are just standard normal PDF for each. For instance,
    \begin{equation*} \rho(z_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-z_1^2/2}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \rho(z_1, z_2, \cdots, z_n) = \frac{}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\,\exp\left( -\frac{z_1^2 + z_2^2 + \cdots + z_n^2}{2} \right) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\,e^{-x/2}. \end{equation*}
    Putting this back in Eq. (1.10.5) gives
    \begin{equation} P( x \le X \le x + dx ) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\,e^{-x/2}\ \left[dz_1dz_2...dz_n \right]_{x = \sum_i z_i^2}.\tag{1.10.6} \end{equation}
    The quantity in the square bracket restricted to the spherical shell is just the volume of this \(n\)-dimensional spherical shell of radius \(r = \sqrt{x}\) and theickness \(dr = d\sqrt{x}\) since \(x\) is the square of the radius as shown in the figure to the right.
    \begin{align*} dV \amp= \frac{2 \pi^{n/2}\,r^{n-1} }{\Gamma(n/2)} dr \\ \amp = \frac{2 \pi^{n/2}\,x^{(n-1)/2} }{\Gamma(n/2)} \frac{dx}{2\sqrt{x}}, \end{align*}
    where \(\Gamma(.)\) is the Gamma/factorial function.
    Substituting the volume of the shell in Eq. (1.10.6) we get
    \begin{equation*} P( x \le X \le x + dx ) = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(n/2)}\,x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-x/2}\, dx. \end{equation*}
    Comparing this with what we expected in (1.10.4), we find the PDF of the \(\chi^2\) variable \(X\) to be
    \begin{equation} \rho_X(x; n) = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(n/2)}\,x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-x/2}.\tag{1.10.7} \end{equation}
    The mean and variance is not that challenging a calulation either if you are not afraid of these integrals. first thing I will do to the PDF is to rewrite in variable \(y=x/2\text{.}\) You can show that
    \begin{equation*} \rho_X(x; n) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2)}\,y^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-y},\quad x = 2y,\ 0 \le y \lt \infty, \end{equation*}
    Now, it is much easier to see the Gamma functions in the integrals when you compute expectation values of powers of \(X\text{.}\) Thus, mean will be
    \begin{align*} \mu \amp = \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2)} \int_{0}^\infty 2y \,y^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-y}\, dy, \text{used }x=2y. \\ \amp= \frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2)} 2\times \Gamma(n/2 + 1) = n. \end{align*}
    The last step follows from the recursion relation of Gamma functions.
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma(a + 1) = a \Gamma(a),\, x\gt 0. \end{equation*}
    Similarly, we get the following expectation value of the square of random variable.
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[X^2] \amp = \frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2)} \int_{0}^\infty 4\,x^2 \,y^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-y}\, dy. \\ \amp= \frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2)} 4\times \Gamma(n/2 + 2) = 2 n\left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right). \end{align*}
    The variance will be
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \left( \mathbb{E}[X] \right)^2 = 2n \left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right) - n^2 = 2n. \end{equation*}
    Figure 1.10.1 shows plots of the PDF \(\chi^2_n(x)\) for various values of \(n\text{.}\) If the plots remind you of Gamma distribution, then you are on the right track, since Chi Square distribution is just a Gamma distribution of \(Y\) with \(Y=X/2\) substitution, i.e., of a scaled chi-squared variable, as we have seen above in our calculations.

    Subsection 1.10.2 t-ditribution

    The Student t-distribution is a probability distribution used in statistics to model data when sample sizes are small or population variance is unknown as we will see when we study Inferential Statistics. Here we study the math and characteristics of the distribution itself. “Student” is the pseudonym of William Sealy Gosset who published it in 1908.
    The \(t\)-random variable is constructed from two other random variables, \(Z\) and \(Y\text{,}\) with
    \begin{equation*} Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad Y\sim \chi^2_n;\quad -\infty\lt Z \lt \infty,\, 0\lt Y \lt \infty. \end{equation*}
    Then, we define the \(t\)-random variable by
    \begin{equation} T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{Y/n}}.\tag{1.10.8} \end{equation}
    You can see that the square root in the denominator is trying to modify the scale of \(Z\) by sampled standard deviation. We will discuss these ideas in a later section. Here we simply ask: what is the PDF of \(T\) given the PDF’s of \(Z\) and \(Y\text{?}\) The ansewer turns out to be the \(t\)-distribution.
    \begin{equation} \rho_T(t; n) = \frac{ \Gamma \left( \frac{n+1}{2} \right) }{ \sqrt{n\pi} \Gamma(n/2)} \left( \frac{t^2}{n} + 1 \right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}.\tag{1.10.9} \end{equation}

    Derivation.

    Strategy: The transformation we seek in Eq. (1.10.8) is a from two-variable space of \(Y\) and \(Z\) to one variable \(T\text{.}\) The trick is to include a fake second variable, which is just the same as \(Y\text{,}\) which we call \(W\text{.}\) Thus, a point \((y,z)\) in \((Y,Z)\) space will have the following relation to a point \((w,t)\) in \((W,T)\) space.
    \begin{align} \amp y = w \tag{1.10.10}\\ \amp z = t \sqrt{y/n} = t \sqrt{w/n}\tag{1.10.11} \end{align}
    So, we will have a transformation for \((Y,Z)\) space to \((W, T)\) space obtained a joint PDF in \((W,T)\text{.}\) It turns out that margnining out \(W\text{,}\) i.e., integrating out \(w\)m> in this joint PDF is rather straight forward and leads to the desired \(\rho_T(t)\text{.}\)
    Since we already know the PDFs of \(Y\) and \(Z\) and since we are assuming they are independent variables, their joint probability distribution is just the product their separate PDFs.
    \begin{align} \rho_{YZ}(y,z) \amp = \rho_Y(y)\;\rho_Z(z)\tag{1.10.12}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(n/2)}\,y^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-y/2}\times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}.\tag{1.10.13} \end{align}
    Now, to go from \((Y,Z)\) to \((W,T)\) space, we need to preserve probabilities in infintesimal volumes.
    \begin{align*} \rho_{W,T}(w,t)\, dw dt \amp = \rho_{Y,Z}(y,z)\, dy dz\\ \amp = \rho_{Y,Z}(w,z(w,t))\, J\, dw dt, \end{align*}
    where \(J\) is the Jacobian of transformation given by the following determinant.
    \begin{equation*} J = \begin{vmatrix} \frac{\partial y}{\partial w} \amp \frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} \amp \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} \end{vmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The partial derivatives in the Jacobian are easily obtained from Eqs. (1.10.10) and (1.10.11).
    \begin{align*} \amp \frac{\partial y}{\partial w}= 1,\quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial t}= 0 \\ \amp \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}= \frac{t}{2\sqrt{n w}},\quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t}= \sqrt{ \frac{w}{n} } \end{align*}
    Therefore, the Jacobian is
    \begin{equation*} J = \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 0\\ \frac{t}{2\sqrt{n w}} \amp \sqrt{ \frac{w}{n} } \end{vmatrix} = \sqrt{ \frac{w}{n} }. \end{equation*}
    Hence,
    \begin{equation*} \rho_{W,T}(w,t)\, dw dt = \rho_{Y,Z}(w,z(w,t))\, \sqrt{ \frac{w}{n} }\, dw dt \end{equation*}
    All we need now is to express \rho_{Y,Z} in terms of \(w\) and \(t\) by substitutions in Eq. (1.10.13).
    \begin{equation*} \rho_{Y,Z}(y(w), z(w,t)) = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(n/2)}\,w^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-w/2}\times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-t^2w/2n} \end{equation*}
    Notice that when you collect terms containing \(w\text{,}\) they are just what we need for an integral representation of \(\Gamma\) function:
    \begin{equation*} w^{a}\, e^{-b w},\quad a = \frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2},\quad b = \frac{t^2}{2n} + frac{1}{2}. \end{equation*}
    With these \(a\) and \(b\) we can a simple expression for \(\rho_{W,T}(w,t)\, dw dt\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \rho_{W,T}(w,t)\, dw dt = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}} \frac{1}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(n/2)} w^{a}\, e^{-b w}\, dw dt \end{equation*}
    Marginitin out \(w\) requires integration \(w\) from \(0\) to \(\infty\text{.}\) Since
    \begin{align*} \int_0^\infty\, w^{a}\, e^{-b w}\, dw \amp = \frac{1}{b^{a+!}}\int_0^\infty\, x^a\, e^-x\, dx, \quad (x = bw) = \frac{1}{b^{a+1}}\Gamma(a+1)\\ \amp = \Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\, 2^{n/2}\sqrt{2} \left( \frac{t^2}{n} + 1 \right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}} \end{align*}
    Now, we integrate out \(w\) in \(\rho_{W,T}(w,t)\, dw dt\) to get the \(t\)-distribution.That is, from
    \begin{equation*} \rho_T(t) dt = \left( \int_0^\infty \rho_{W,T}(w,t)\, dw \right) dt, \end{equation*}
    afte canceling out the factors of \(2\)’s, we get
    \begin{equation*} \rho_T(t) = \frac{ \Gamma \left( \frac{n+1}{2} \right) }{ \sqrt{n\pi} \Gamma(n/2)} \left( \frac{t^2}{n} + 1 \right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\qquad \blacksquare \end{equation*}
    Figure 1.10.2 displays the t-distribution PDF for various values of the degree of freedom parameter (df) \(n\) and a standard normal, \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{.}\) First thing to notice is that the \(t\)-distribution is symmetric about the mean similar to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{.}\) But, for smaller \(n\) values, the \(t\)-distribution has “fatter tails”, meaning rides above the \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) for larger values of the argument. It is interesting that \(n\approx 30\) is visually almost inditinguishable from the standard normal.
    The mean of \(t\)-distribution is easily calculated by directly evaluating the integral.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[T] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\, t\; \rho_T(t) dt = 0. \end{equation*}
    This is due to the fact that \(\rho_T(t)\) is symmetric in \(t\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \rho_T(-t) = + \rho_T(t), \end{equation*}
    and when you multiply it with \(t\text{,}\) the integrand becomes an odd function of \(t\text{.}\) The integration of an odd function with symmetric limits will be zero.
    A direct calculation of the variance is a little more challenging. It’s just too much algebra to get to the same result. So, I will give you an alternate calaculation based on the fact that the random variables \(Y\) and \(Z\) that go into the defining of \(T\) variable are independent. Recall
    \begin{equation*} T = \frac{\sqrt{n}\;Z}{\sqrt{Y}}. \end{equation*}
    For Variance of \(T\) with mean equalto zero is just
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Var}(T) = \mathbb{E}[T^2]. \end{equation*}
    That amounts to
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Var}(T) =\mathbb{E}_{YZ}\left[\frac{n\;Z^2}{Y}\right] = n\;\mathbb{E}_Z[Z^2]\,\mathbb{E}_Y \left[\frac{1}{Y}\right]. \end{equation*}
    Now, we know that \(\mathbb{E}_Z[Z^2] = 1\) since \(Z\sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{.}\) Hence,
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Var}(T) = n\;\mathbb{E}_Y \left[\frac{1}{Y}\right]. \end{equation*}
    The expectation value on the right side is a much simler Gamma function integral I will leave for you to do.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}_Y \left[\frac{1}{Y}\right] = \frac{1}{n-2},\quad n \ne 2. \end{equation*}
    Also, for \(n=1\text{,}\) you can already show that \(\textrm{Var}(t)\) integral itself divergent. Therefore, we write the variance as
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Var}(T) = \frac{n}{n-2},\quad n\gt 2. \end{equation*}
