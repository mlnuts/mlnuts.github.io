    Section 1.7 Discrete Probability Distributions

    Probability distributions describe how probabilities are spread across values of a random variable. Recall that when the random variable is discrete, we have a finite set of choices for the variable values. One example is a random variable \(X\) that records the outcome of rolls of a six-sided die. This \(X\) will take one of the six values in its sample space \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\text{.}\) The probability for each outcome gives us Probability Mass Function (PMF) of the random variable. Thus, PMF of the die rolls, which may be an unfair die prefering one outcome over another, could be
    \begin{equation*} P(1) = \frac{1}{10}, P(2) = \frac{1}{5}, P(3) = \frac{1}{5}, P(4) = \frac{1}{8}, P(5) = \frac{1}{4}, P(6) = \frac{1}{8}. \end{equation*}
    The sum of probabilities of every unique outcome in the sample space \(\Omega\) must equal 1.
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^6\, P(i) = 1. \end{equation*}
    In this section, we will look at commonly occuring discrete random variables and their PMFs. We defer discussion of examples of continuous random variables and their probability density function (PDF) and cumulative distribution functions (CDF) to the next section.

    Subsection 1.7.1 Bernoulli Distribution

    Tossing of a two-sided cin is a prototypical example of a Bernouli experiment - you get either heads (\(H\)) or tails (\(T\)). Every two-state problem where outcome is uncertain can be mapped to this problem. We can represent the uncertain outcome of the experiment by a random variable \(X\) with two real values \(0\) for "tails" and \(1\) for "tails". That is, the sample space will be just
    \begin{equation} \Omega = \{ X=0, X=1\}\tag{1.7.1} \end{equation}
    We call such random variables Bernoulli variables. We specify the PMF of Bernouli disrtibution by stating the probability of the two unique events with letter \(p\) for \(P(X=1)\text{.}\)
    \begin{align} P(X=1) \amp = p \tag{1.7.2}\\ P(X=0) \amp = q \tag{1.7.3} \end{align}
    From normalization of PMF, it’s immediately known that
    \begin{equation*} q = 1 - p. \end{equation*}
    The separate listing of the probability of the two values of \(X\) in Eqs. (1.7.2) and (1.7.3) can actually be written more conveniently in one formula.
    \begin{equation} P(X=x) = p^x\:(1-p)^{1-x}.\tag{1.7.4} \end{equation}
    From this formula, you will get \(P(X=1)\) and \(P(X=0)\) by substitting \(x=1\) and \(x=9\text{,}\) respectively.
    Graphically, Bernoulli distribution are usually plotted as a bar graph. To the right is a plot as bars with a dot at the top of the bar so that you can read the \(p\) and \(q\) values more easily. Figure to the side shows an illustration with \(p=0.6\) for \(X=1\text{,}\) and of course, \(q= 0.4\) for \(X=0\text{.}\)
    The mean of variable \(X\) is nothing but weighed average of each value of \(X\) weighted with its probability. This is also the expectation value \(\mathbb{E}[X]\text{.}\) We also represent mean of distributions by symbol \(\mu\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} \mu \equiv \mathbb{E}[X] = 0 \times P(X=0) + 1\times P(X=1) = 0 + p = p\tag{1.7.5} \end{equation}
    Similarly we can find the expectation value of any power of \(X\text{.}\) For instance, the expectation value of the \(n^\text{th}\) power of \(X\) will be
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[X^n] = 0^n \times P(X=0) + 1^n\times P(X=1) = 0 + p = p\tag{1.7.6} \end{equation}
    Thus, variance, which is represented by \(\sigma^2\text{,}\) where \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, will be
    \begin{equation} \textrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \left( \mathbb{E}[X]\right)^2 = p - (p)^2 = p(1-p).\tag{1.7.7} \end{equation}
    Therefore, standard deviation, \(\sigma\text{,}\) of Bernoulli variable is
    \begin{equation} \sigma = \sqrt{ \text{Var}(X) } = \sqrt{p(1-p)}.\tag{1.7.8} \end{equation}

    Subsection 1.7.2 Binomial Distribution

    Imagine a collection of \(n\) identical coins. A Binomial experiment on this system consists of tossing each coin once and counting the total number of Heads or Tails you got. For instance, for a \(10\)-coin experiment, you might get no Heads at all or 1 Heads and 9 Tails, or 2 Heads and 8 Tails, etc. Record how many Heads you got in this trial, say you got 3 Heads. Now, let’s repeat the experiment. In the second trial, you might get a different number of Heads, say this time you got 8 Heads. That is, total number of heads in this experiment is an example of a Binomial random variable.
    If you repeat this experiment many times, you can see a pattern emerge among the values of the random variable. Table Table 1.7.1 shows results 2000 trials of this experiment, organized un a table, called a frequency table. You have more chances of the results to be somewhere in the middle of the range of values than on either exremes.
    In general, a Binomial random variable counts the total number of success (or failure) in a fixed number of Bernoulli trials. The resulting probabilities make up the Probability Mass Function (PMF) of Binomial disrtibution. Since a Binomial experiment is a collection of identical and independent Bernoulli trials, it is possible to deduce a mathematical formula of the Binomial PMF based on the PMF of the Bernoulli.
    Let \(p\) be the probability in each Bernoulli trial and let there be \(n\) Bernoulli trials in each Binomial experiment. In each trial of the Binomial experiment you will have a sequence of \(n\) Bernoulli outcomes \(H\) (for success) or \(T\) (for failure).
    \begin{equation*} \text{One Binomial Trial: } THTTTTHHTHHHTH...H\quad \text{(a total of N symbols.)} \end{equation*}
    Suppose this sequence has \(m\) Heads (\(H\)) and \((n-m)\) Tails (\(T\)). The probability of this sequence of independent Bernoulli results will be just a product of \(m\) factors of \(p\) for each \(H\) and \((n-m)\) factors for \((1-p)\) for each \(T\text{,}\) which will give us
    \begin{equation*} p^m\,(1-p)^{n-m}. \end{equation*}
    But the \(H\) and \(T\) could have occurred in any order and we would still have the same \(m\) Heads.To get the probability of getting a total of \(m\) Heads in any order, we must add up probabilities from all those other permutations as well. That should just mean we need to multiply the above one-order probability by the number of permutations of \(m\) \(H\)’s in \(n\) slots and the remainder by \(T\)’s. That turns out to be the Binomial coefficient, and hence the name Binomial distribution.
    \begin{equation} P(X = m; n, p) = \frac{n!}{m! (n-m)!}\,p^m\,(1-p)^{n-m}.\tag{1.7.9} \end{equation}
    Note that this distribution has two fixed parameters, the number of independent Bernoulli trials \(n\) in each Binomial trial and \(p\text{,}\) the probability of “success” in each Bernoulli trial. I will like to simplify this notation, by writing \(P\) with subscripts \(n,p\) and keep the \(X\) when necessary.
    \begin{equation} P_{n,p}(X = m) = \frac{n!}{m! (n-m)!}\,p^m\,(1-p)^{n-m}.\tag{1.7.10} \end{equation}
    It emphasizes the fact that even for the same \(p\text{,}\) you have different Binomial experiments for different values of \(n\text{.}\) For instance, in Table Table 1.7.1, if you had conducted the experiment with \(20\) Bernoulli trials in each Binomial experiment, instead of \(10\text{,}\) you would have gotten much different probabilities for \(P(0),\ P(1), P(2), \cdots\text{.}\) This is illustrated in the following figure, Figure 1.7.2.
    Following code was used to create the plot above.
    import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import binom

# Parameters
p = 0.5  # probability of success
N1 = 20  # number of trials for first distribution
N2 = 10  # number of trials for second distribution

# Support for each distribution
x1 = np.arange(0, N1+1)
x2 = np.arange(0, N2+1)

# PMFs
pmf1 = binom.pmf(x1, N1, p)
pmf2 = binom.pmf(x2, N2, p)

# Plot
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8,5))

# Binomial N1
ax.vlines(x1, 0, pmf1, colors='blue', lw=2, label=f'Bin{n1}')
ax.plot(x1, pmf1, 'o', color='blue')

# Binomial N2
ax.vlines(x2, 0, pmf2, colors='orange', lw=2, label=f'Bin{n2}')
ax.plot(x2, pmf2, 'o', color='orange')

# Labels and grid
ax.set_title(f'Binomial Distribution PMFs (p={p})')
ax.set_xlabel('Number of Successes')
ax.set_ylabel('Probability')
ax.grid(axis='y', linestyle='--', alpha=0.6)
ax.legend()

plt.show()
    Another way to improve your intuition about the Binomial distribution is to look at the impact of changing \(p\) value for the Bernoulli trials themselves - what impact do they have on a \(10\)-Binomial? It is shown in Figure 1.7.3. These plots show that low \(p (0.2)\) skews the PMF toward fewer successes; \(p = 0.5\) produces a symmetric distribution centered at \(n/2\text{;}\) high \(p (0.8)\) skews toward more successes.
    For doing analytical calculations with the Binomial distribution, it is important to recall the following algebraic identity, called Binomial expansion.
    \begin{equation} (a + b)^n = \sum_{m=0}^{n}\, \frac{n!}{m! (n-m)!}\,a^m\,b^{n-m}.\tag{1.7.11} \end{equation}
    Using this it is straightforward to show that Binomial distribution is normalized properly since
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=0}^{n}\,P_{n,p}(X = m) = \sum_{m=0}^{n}\,\frac{n!}{m! (n-m)!}\,p^m\,(1-p)^{n-m} = \left[ p + (1-p)\right]^n = 1. \end{equation*}
    The mean \(\mu\) of the Binomial random variable \(X\) can be obtained by weighing each value of \(X \in \{0, 1, 2, \cdots, N\} \) by the corresponding probability. This is just the expectation value of the variable \(X\) itself.
    \begin{equation} \mu = \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{m=0}^{n}\, m \, P_{n,p}(X = m) = N\,p.\tag{1.7.12} \end{equation}
    A simple method of showing the result involves taking an appropriate derivative appropriately.
    \begin{align*} \sum_{m=0}^{n}\,m\,\frac{n!}{m! (n-m)!}\,p^m\,(1-p)^{n-m} \amp = \left[ p\frac{d}{dp}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\,\frac{n!}{m! (n-m)!}\,p^m\,q^{n-m} \right]_{q=1-p}\\ \amp = \left[ p\frac{d}{dp}(p + q)^n \right]_{q=1-p} \\ \amp = \left[p n(p + q)^{n-1} \right]_{q=1-p} = N\, p. \end{align*}
    The variance is similarly shown to be
    \begin{equation} \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \left( \mathbb{E}[X] \right)^2 = N\,p\,(1-p).\tag{1.7.13} \end{equation}
    And, the standard deviation \(\sigma\) is just the square root.
    \begin{equation} \sigma = \sqrt{\textrm{Var}(X) } = \sqrt{n\,p\,(1-p)}.\tag{1.7.14} \end{equation}

    Subsubsection 1.7.2.1 Average of Random Variables

    If we divide the Binomial random variable by \(n\text{,}\) the number of independent Bernoulli trials in the Binomial experiment, we will get the average of those Bernoulli trials. Let \(X_1,\ X_2, \ \cdots\ , X_n\) denote the Bernoulli random variables that make up the Binomial experiment. The average, denoted by \(\overline{X_n}\) will just be
    \begin{equation*} \overline{X_n} = \frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}{n}. \end{equation*}
    This random variable is called sample mean. From the values the Binomial random value in the numerator, it is clear that the samle mean will be one of the following values in each Binomial trial.
    \begin{equation*} \overline{X_n} \in \{ 0, \frac{1}{n}, \frac{2}{n}, \cdots, \frac{n}{n}=1 \} \end{equation*}
    A good way to think about the sample mean is that you have some elementary experiment whose outcome is \(1\) or \(0\) (the component Bernoulli trials) and you would like to find out \(p\text{.}\) So, you set up \(n\) trials and average them to get a better estimation.
    With \(p\) being probability of any of the individual Bernoulli variables to produce a success, i.e., \(p = P(X_i = 1)\) for every one of the \(i = 1, 2, \cdots, N\text{,}\) it is immediately follows that expectation value of the sample mean variable is just \(p\text{,}\) independent of \(n\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[\overline{X_n}] = p. \end{equation*}
    Due to the scaling of the Binomial variable to get the sample mean variable, the variance of the sample mean variable is \(1/N^2\) times the variance of the Binomial variable.
    \begin{equation*} \text{Var}(\overline{X_n}) = \frac{ \sqrt{n p(1-p)} }{n^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}, \end{equation*}
    which translates to the standard deviation that goes as \(\sim 1/\sqrt{n}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sigma = \sqrt{ \text{Var}(\overline{X_n}) } = \frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{\sqrt{n}}. \end{equation*}
    As you include increasing number of Bernoulli trials in each Binomial experiment, you estimate of the mean of the sample mean shrink around the true value \(p\text{.}\) Where does this result matter?
    • Estimation: In statistics, we often estimate \(p\) by \(\langle \overline{X_n} \rangle\) from data.
    • Interpretation: If you run multiple experiments, your average success rate will be centered at \(p\) and become more concentrated as \(n\) grows since the standard deviation drops as \(\sim 1/\sqrt{n}\text{.}\) This is illustrated in Figure 1.7.4.
    • Connection to the Central Limit Theorem: For large \(n\text{,}\) it can be shown that the probability distribution of the random variable \(\overline{X_n}\) tends to become Gaussian with the mean \(p\) and variance \(p(1-p)/N\text{.}\) We write this as
      \begin{equation*} \overline{X_n} \approx \text{Gaussian}\left( p, \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\right), \end{equation*}
      even though each \(X_i\) is a discrete random variable. This goes by the name Central Limit Theorem, which we will discuss in another section.

    Subsection 1.7.3 Multinomial/Categorical Distribution

    Multinomial distribution is like the binomial’s cooler cousin who handles multiple categories at once. Imagine rolling a die, sorting emails into spam, promo, or important, or predicting labels in a machine learning classifier. This distribution is your go-to for modeling counts across several outcomes. Why’s it a big deal in machine learning? It’s everywhere! In natural language processing, it powers things like bag-of-words models for text. In classification, it’s behind softmax outputs, where your model predicts probabilities over multiple classes. Plus, it’s key for understanding likelihoods in algorithms like Naive Bayes or evaluating models with metrics like cross-entropy loss. If you’re working with categorical data in ML, the multinomial is your best friend.

    Definition 1.7.5. Definition of the Multinomial Distribution.

    Picture this: you run \(n\) independent trials, each with \(k\) possible outcomes, where outcome \(i\) has probability \(p_i\) (and \(\sum_{i=1}^k p_i = 1\)). Let us use a compact notation to represent variables for the \(k\) outcomes as a vector: \(\vec{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_k)\) count how many times each outcome happens. Then \(\vec{X} \sim \text{Multinomial}(n, \vec{p})\text{,}\) with probability:
    \begin{equation*} P(\vec{X} = \vec{x}) = \frac{n!}{x_1! x_2! \dots x_k!} p_1^{x_1} p_2^{x_2} \dots p_k^{x_k}, \end{equation*}
    where \(x_i\) are non-negative integers summing to \(n\text{.}\) Each \(X_i\) is like a binomial, but they’re tied together since \(\sum x_i = n\text{.}\)

    Example 1.7.6. Visualizing with a Bar Plot.

    Let’s make this fun with a Python plot. Let’s simulate 1000 rolls of a fair six-sided die (\(k=6\text{,}\) \(p_i = 1/6\)) and show the counts in a bar chart, with each bar labeled with its frequency. Run this code to see how the counts wiggle around the expected \(n/k \approx 166.67\text{.}\) 
    import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Parameters
n = 1000  # number of trials
p = np.array([1/6] * 6)  # fair die probabilities
labels = ['1', '2', '3', '4', '5', '6']

# Generate one sample
sample = np.random.multinomial(n, p)
print(sample)

# Plot
bars = plt.bar(labels, sample, color='skyblue')
plt.xlabel('Die Faces')
plt.ylabel('Counts')
plt.title('Multinomial Sample: 1000 Rolls of a Fair Die')

# Add frequency labels on top of each bar
for bar, count in zip(bars, sample):
    plt.text(bar.get_x() + bar.get_width() / 2, bar.get_height(),
            str(count), ha='center', va='bottom')

plt.show()
    Figure 1.7.7 shows the results of runninng this program once. You would have to run it a few times to see the randomness, or bump up \(n\) to watch the counts stabilize.
    The mean, denoted by \(p_i\) and variance, denoted by \(\Sigma_{ii}\) (the use of two subscripts will become clear below when we discuss Covariance), of a multinomial variable, i.e., one of the \(X_i\) in its definition, are
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \sum_{x_1,x_2,...,x_k} x_i P(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_k) = n\,p_i \\ \amp \textrm{Var}(X_i) = \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] - \left( \mathbb{E}[X_i] \right)^2 = n\,p_i\,(1-p_i) \end{align*}
    The covariance among the different outcome variables is important here since different \(X_i\)’s are not independent. In fact, covariance is given by
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Cov}(X_i, X_{j\ne i}) \equiv \mathbb{E}\left( X_i\,X_j\right) - \mathbb{E}\left( X_i \right)\, \mathbb{E}\left( X_j \right) = - n p_i\,p_j. \end{equation*}
    These are off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix, usually denoted by a bold Greek letter, \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) with
    \begin{align*} \amp\mathbf{\Sigma}_{ii} = n p_i(1-p_i) \\ \amp\mathbf{\Sigma}_{ij^{i \ne j}} = - p_ip_j \end{align*}
    The off-diagonals are negative because of th constraint among the variables.
    \begin{equation*} X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_k = n. \end{equation*}

    Derivation of the Formula for the Mean.

    Derivation of these formulas is instructive. So, I will include them here. Let’s take the class labeled \(i=1\) for specificity.
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[X_1] \amp = \sum_{x_1=0}^{n} \frac{n}{x_1!} x_1 p_1^{x_1}\, \sum_{x_2=0}^{n} \frac{1}{x_2!} p_2^{x_2}\cdots \sum_{x_k=0}^{n} \frac{1}{x_k!} p_k^{x_k}\, \text{ with } \sum_{i=1}^k x_i = n. \end{align*}
    Now, let us lump together the outcomes \(2, 3, \cdots, k\) as \(\bar{1}\text{.}\) Let the probability for \(\bar{1}\) be \(p_\bar{1}\text{.}\) Then, the sum with constraint already built in will be just the Binomial distribution between the outcomes \(1\) and \(\bar{1}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X_1] = \sum_{x_1=0}^{n} \frac{n}{x_1! x_\bar{1}} x_1 p_1^{x_1} p_\bar{1}^{x_\bar{1}!},\quad x_\bar{1} = \sum_{i=2}^k x_i. \end{equation*}
    It’s easy to do this sum with the trick of taking derivative to bring down a factor of \(x_1\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X_1] = p_1\frac{\partial}{\partial p_1}\left( \sum_{x_1=0}^{n} \frac{n}{x_1! x_\bar{1}!} p_1^{x_1} p_\bar{1}^{x_\bar{1}}\right) \end{equation*}
    The sum is just a binomial expression with \(p_1 + p_\bar{1} = 1\text{,}\) which we use at the end.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X_1] = p_1\frac{\partial}{\partial p_1}(p_1 + p_\bar{1})^n = n\,p_1 (p_1 + p_\bar{1})^{n-1} = n\,p_1. \end{equation*}

    Derivation of the Formula for the Variance.

    For variance, we need to compute the second moment of the distribution. We use the same trick as for the first moment. Again, just lookig at \(i=1\) will be enough.
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[X_1^2] \amp = \left(p_1 \frac{\partial}{\partial p_1} \right) \left(p_1 \frac{\partial}{\partial p_1} \right) \left(p_1 + p_\bar{1} \right)^{n}\\ \amp = \left(p_1 \frac{\partial}{\partial p_1} \right) \left[ n p_1\left(p_1 + p_\bar{1} \right)^{n} \right] \\ \amp = p_1\left( n + n(n-1)p_1\right),\quad (\text{ using }p_1 + p_\bar{1} = 1) \end{align*}
    Hence,
    \begin{equation*} \text{Var}(X_1) = p_1\left( n + n(n-1)p_1\right) - (n\,p_1)^2 = n\,p_1 (1-p_1). \end{equation*}

    Using Indicator Variables to Prove Variance Formual.

    There is a very useful method for doing calculations in multinomial variables (and other places as well). Here, we have \(n\) experiments, each gives outcome in \(k\) possibilities. So, we can intriduce \(n\times k\) Bernouli random variables \(Y_{t,i}\text{,}\) where \(t=1,2,\cdots, n\) and \(i=1,2,\cdots,k\) by the following.
    \begin{equation*} Y_{t,i} = \begin{cases} 1 \amp \text{ if trial } t \text{ has outcome } i\\ 0 \amp \text{ otherwise} \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Note that since \(Y_{t,i}\) is a Bernoulli trial with probability \(p_i\text{,}\) we will have easy answer:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[Y_{t,i}] = p_i,\quad \text{Var}(Y_{t,i}) = p_i(1-p_i) \end{equation*}
    Since \(X_i\) is the total number of trials that had outcome \(i\text{,}\) we will have
    \begin{equation*} X_i = \sum_{t=1}^n\,Y_{t,i}. \end{equation*}
    Now, it is almost trivial to get the mean, variance, and the off-diagonal covariances.
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[X_i] \amp = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{t=1}^n\, Y_{t,i} \right] = \sum_{t=1}^n \mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t,i} \right] \\ \amp = \sum_{t=1}^n\,p_i = p_i \sum_{t=1}^n\,1 = n\,p_i. \end{align*}
    \begin{align*} \text{Var}(X_i) \amp = \text{Var}\left( \sum_{t=1}^n\, Y_{t,i} \right) = \sum_{t=1}^n \text{Var}\left( Y_{t,i} \right) = n\,p_i (1-p_i) \end{align*}
    For \(i \ne j\) we have a do a little extra work, but still trivial.
    \begin{align*} \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) \amp = \mathbb{E}[X_i X_j] - \mathbb{E}[X_i]\, \mathbb{E}[X_i] \\ \amp = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^n Y_{t,i}\, \sum_{s=1}^n Y_{s,j}\right] - n^2 p_i p_j \\ \amp = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^n Y_{t,i}\,Y_{t,j} + \sum_{t \ne s} Y_{t,i}\,Y_{s,j} \right] - n^2 p_i p_j\\ \amp = \sum_{t=1}^n\mathbb{E}\left[ Y_{t,i}\,Y_{t,j} \right] + \sum_{t \ne s} \mathbb{E}\left[ Y_{t,i}\,Y_{s,j} \right] - n^2 p_i p_j \end{align*}
    Now, notice the first sum. Since in the same trial, you will get a unique outcome, either \(i\) or \(j \ne i\text{.}\) Hence, one of these \(Y\) values will be zero in every term of the first term. That will make the first term identically zero.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}\left[ Y_{t,i}\,Y_{t,j} \right] = 0. \end{equation*}
    In the second sum, the \(Y_{t,i}\) and \(Y_{s\ne t,j}\) are from different trials, so they must be independent.
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}\left[ Y_{t,i}\,Y_{s,j} \right] = \mathbb{E}\left[ Y_{t,i} \right]\, \mathbb{E}\left[ Y_{s,j} \right] = p_i\,p_j \end{equation*}
    This sums over trials \(t\ne s\text{,}\) each of which run from \(1\) to \(n\text{,}\) so we have \(n(n-1)\) terms in total.
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{t \ne s}\, 1 = n(n-1). \end{equation*}
    Hence, the off-diagonal covariance elements are
    \begin{equation*} \text{Cov}(X_i, X_{j\ne i}) = n(n-1)\,p_i p_j n^2 p_i p_j = -n\,p_i p_i. \end{equation*}
    The aymptotic behavior of multinomial distribution when \(n\) is huge is of considerable importance for powering tests like chi-squared for goodness of fit test, among other uses. So, I will just state here the results and use them later. By the multivariate central limit theorem, the proportions \(\vec{X}/n\) get close to a multivariate normal in distribution sense:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{n} \left( \frac{\vec{X}}{n} - \vec{p} \right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{n}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{\Sigma}), \end{equation*}
    where \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) has diagonals \(p_i(1 - p_i)\) and off-diagonals \(-p_i p_j\) for \(i \neq j\text{.}\)

    Subsection 1.7.4 Poisson Distribution

    The Poisson distribution models the number of times an event occurs in a fixed interval of time or space, given that:
    • Events occur independently.
    • Events happen at a constant average rate, usually denoted by Greek letter lambda \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    • Two events cannot occur at the exact same instant. That means we are usually interested in events that are rare within the interval we choose to work with so that it can be safely assumed that two events do not coincide.
    A Poisson random variable \(X\) can take any non-negative integer values since it’s just a count. The probability mass function for a Poisson random variable will give probabilities for \(X = k\) for each non-negative value \(k\) for a constant average rate \(\lambda\) is given by
    \begin{equation} P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda}\, \frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}.\tag{1.7.15} \end{equation}
    It is obviously normalized since
    \begin{equation*} e^{\lambda} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\, \frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!} \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=0}^\infty\, P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda}\times e^\lambda = 1. \end{equation*}
    The mean of Poisson distribution is the average count, \lambda.
    \begin{equation} \langle X \rangle = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\, k\, P(X = k) = \lambda,\tag{1.7.16} \end{equation}
    as we can show by the following calculations.
    \begin{align*} \langle X \rangle \amp = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\, k\, P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\, k\, \frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!} \\ \amp = e^{-\lambda}\, \left( \lambda\,\frac{d}{d\lambda} \right)\sum_{k=0}^\infty\, \frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!} = e^{-\lambda}\, \left( \lambda\,\frac{d}{d\lambda} \right)\, e^\lambda = \lambda. \end{align*}
    The variance of Poisson distribution is similarly shown to be also \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \text{Var}(X) \amp = \langle X^2 \rangle - \langle X \rangle^2\\ \amp = (\lambda + \lambda^2) - \lambda^2 = \lambda. \end{align*}
    where the missing steps are left for the student to practice, using the same type of argument as introducing \(\lambda\,(d/d\lambda)\) operators appropriately.
    A mathematically interesting result is that in an appropriate limit, a Binomial distribution can be shown to become same as Poisson distribution. I will just state the result without giving you the detailed calculations. (hint: You can get \(k\) factors of \(n\) from \(n!/(n-k)!\)).
    \begin{equation*} \frac{n!}{k! (n-k)!}\, p^k\, (1-p)^{n-k} \xrightarrow{n\rightarrow \infty,\ p\rightarrow 0,\ np=\lambda, \text{fixed}} e^{-\lambda}\,\frac{\lambda^k}{k!} \end{equation*}
    Example Radioactivity is one of the classic and most intuitive real-life examples of the Poisson distribution. Let’s look at it a little closely. Radioactive decay is a random process. Each atom has a constant probability of decaying in a fixed time interval. The decays are:
    • Independent (one decay does not affect another).
    • Rare events relative to the huge number of atoms.
    • Occurring with a constant average rate.
    These aspects make the Poisson distribution a perfect model for studying the statistics of radioactivity.
    Suppose we measure the number of particles emitted from a radioactive source in 10-second intervals. From past experiments, we know that the detector records on average 3 decays per 10 seconds. So, per second, we expect on-average \(0.3\) decays.
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = 0.3.\ \ \text{(per second)} \end{equation*}
    That completely specifies the Poisson distribution. Therefore, we can immediately calculate all sorts of things for the phenomenon. For instance, probability of seeing exactly \(0\) decays in \(1\) seconds will be
    \begin{equation*} P(X=0) = e^{-\lambda}\,\frac{\lambda^0}{0!} = e^{-0.3} \approx 0.7408. \end{equation*}
    Probability of exactly 3 decays in a second will be
    \begin{equation*} P(X=0) = e^{-\lambda}\,\frac{\lambda^3}{3!} = e^{-0.3}\times \frac{0.3^3}{3!} \approx 0.0033. \end{equation*}
    Now, for a trick question. What will be the probability of 10 decays in one minute? Well, we will convert our lambda per second to a new lambda per minute. Let’s label lambda’s by the intervals they refer to.
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_{60} = 60 * \lambda_{1} = 60 \times 0.3 = 18. \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} P_{60}(X=10) = e^{-\lambda_{60}}\, \frac{\lambda^{10}}{10!} \approx 0.014. \end{equation*}
    A visual representation of the PMF often helps to build intuition. A simple program in Python can be used to to do that. The plot with \(\lambda = 3\) is shown in Figure 1.7.8.
    import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import poisson

# Average decay rate
lam = 3  

# Range of possible counts
x = np.arange(0, 15)
pmf = poisson.pmf(x, lam)

plt.figure(figsize=(8,5))
plt.vlines(x, 0, pmf, colors='darkred', lw=2, alpha=0.7, label=f'λ={lam}')
plt.plot(x, pmf, 'o', color='black', markersize=5)

plt.xlabel('Number of Decays in One Interval')
plt.ylabel('Probability P(X=k)')
plt.title('Poisson Distribution of Radioactive Decays (λ=3)')
plt.grid(axis='y', linestyle='--', alpha=0.6)
plt.legend()

plt.show()

    Subsubsection 1.7.4.1 Poisson Process

    A Poisson process is a stochastic process used to model the occurrence of events that happen independently and at a constant average rate over time or space.
    Formally, a Poisson process is a counting process \(( \{X(t), t \ge 0\} )\text{,}\) where \(X(t)\) represents the number of events that have occurred up to time \(t\text{,}\) and it satisfies the following properties:
    1. Initial Condition: \(( X(0) = 0 )\text{,}\) meaning the process starts with no events at time zero.
    2. Independent Increments: The number of events in non-overlapping time intervals is independent. For example, the number of events in \((t_1, t_2]\) is independent of the number in \((t_3, t_4]\) if the intervals do not overlap.
    3. Stationary Increments: The number of events in a time interval of length \(t \text{,}\) i.e., \(X(s+t) - X(s) \text{,}\) depends only on the length \(t \) and not on the starting point \(s \text{.}\)
    4. Poisson Distribution: The number of events in any interval of length \(t \) follows a Poisson distribution with mean \(\lambda t \text{,}\) where \(\lambda > 0 \) is the rate parameter (average number of events per unit time). The probability of \(k \) events in an interval of length \(t \) is:
      \begin{equation*} P(X(t) = k) = \frac{(\lambda t)^k e^{-\lambda t}}{k!}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots \end{equation*}
    5. no Simultaneous Events: The probability of two or more events occurring at exactly the same time is negligible (technically, the probability of multiple events in an infinitesimally small interval is zero).
    Examples:
    1. Queueing Systems: Customers arriving at a store at an average rate of \(\lambda = 5 \) customers per hour.
    2. Telecommunications: Phone calls arriving at a call center with a constant average rate.
    3. Reliability: Failures of a machine occurring randomly at an average rate of \(\lambda = 0.01 \) failures per hour.
    4. Traffic: Cars passing a checkpoint on a highway at a constant average rate.
