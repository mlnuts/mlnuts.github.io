    Section 1.8 Continuous Probability Distributions

    Continuous random variables take values on an interval of the real line. Unlike discrete random variables, we do not assign probability to single points (which would be zero). Instead, we assign probability to intervals, using probability density functions (PDFs), denoted by \(\rho\text{.}\) For a random variable \(X\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} P( x \le X \le x + dx ) = \rho(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Note that the PDF must be a positive semi-definite for probability to be positive semidefinite and sum (integral here) of the probabilities must add to \(1\) over the entire real line.
    \begin{equation*} \rho(x) \ge 0,\quad \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\rho(x)\,dx = 1. \end{equation*}
    To faciliate analytical work, we introduce a cumulative distribution function (CDF), denoted by \(F\text{,}\) that gives probability of the event that \(X \le x\) for some particular \(x\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} F(x) = P( X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x\, \rho(x')\,dx'. \end{equation*}
    Clearly, normalization of probability requires that
    \begin{equation*} F (+\infty) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\, \rho(x)\,dx = 1. \end{equation*}
    From the definition of \(F(x)\) and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we have the following relation also.
    \begin{equation*} \rho(x) = \frac{dF}{dx} \end{equation*}
    Although, we have not attached the random variable to the symbols \(P\text{,}\) \(\rho\text{,}\) \(F\text{,}\) and \(F^{-1}\text{,}\) you will find the following notations in other places.
    \begin{equation*} P_X(x),\ \rho_X(x),\ \F_X(x). \end{equation*}
    If your distribution is over a conditonal random variable, say the variable \(X\text{,}\) conditioned over some specific value of another random variable \(Y=y_j\text{,}\) then notation may be
    \begin{equation*} P_{X\mid y_j}(x),\ \rho_{X\mid y_j}(x),\ F_{X\mid y_j}(x). \end{equation*}
    In this section, we will discuss three distributions that are important for ML use.
    • Uniform: All values equally likely
    • Normal (Gaussian): Bell-shaped curve
    • Exponential: Time until an event occurs
    There are, of course, other distributions such as beta and gamma distributions that are also in common use. By presenting just these three here, I hope to give the reader enough feel for what to look for when studying other distributions.
    Inverse CDF:
    Another function of interest when studying continuous random variables is the inverse cumulative distribution function. It maps a probability \(p \in [0,1]\) to the value \(x \in \mathbb{R}\text{,}\) the real line.
    We will denote the inverse CDF by \(F^{-1}\) since we are denoting CDF by \(F\) except for the standard Normal distribution, \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{.}\) For\(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{,}\) the CDF is tranditionally denoted by the Greek letter phi \((\Phi)\) and inverse by \(\Phi^{-1}\text{.}\) The symbol \(F^{-1}\) (or \(\Phi^{-1}\)) does not mean \(1/F\) (or \(1/\Phi\)); it’s just a symbol for inverse operation of CDF.
    \begin{equation*} F^{-1}\left( F(x) \right) = x. \end{equation*}
    Note that inverse of a CDF is a function of probability since it maps a probability to the value of random variable on the real line. As a result, it is often used for generating samples according to a distribution of interest for particular application. Say we want \(m\) samples of a random variable \(X\) that is distributed according a Gaussian distribution, \(\mathbb{N}\)(\mu,\sigma^2), i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} X \sim \mathbb{N}(\mu,\sigma^2). \end{equation*}
    Then, we just find the inverse CDF over \(m\) values of probability distributed uniformly and randomly in the range \([0,1]\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} p_\text{to_sample} = \{ p_1, p_2, \cdots, p_m \}. \end{equation*}
    Then, we get the corresponding \(x\)-samples by using the inverse CDF of the distribution,
    \begin{equation*} x_1 = F^{-1}(p_1),\ x_2 = F^{-1}(p_2), \cdots, x_m \amp F^{-1}(p_m). \end{equation*}

    Subsection 1.8.1 Uniform Distribution

    When a random variable \(X\) is equally likely to take any value between two real numbers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) we say that the distribution is uniform between these values. The distribution is usually designated by \(U(a,b)\) with \(a \lt b\text{,}\) andwe say that
    \begin{equation*} X \sim U(a,b), \end{equation*}
    which is read as \(X\) “is distributed as” \(U(a,b)\text{.}\) Since \(U(a,b)\) is uniform between \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) it’s probability interpretation will be
    \begin{equation} P(x \le X \le x + dx) \equiv \rho(x)\, dx = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{b-a}\, dx \amp \text{if } a \le x \le b \\ 0 \amp \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\tag{1.8.1} \end{equation}
    where the part without the \(dx\) is the PDF of the distribution, rho, \(\rho\text{.}\)
    The mean value of the uniform distribution will clearly be half way between \(a\) and \(b\) as can easily be verified by performing the simple integral for the expecation value of \(X\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{a}^{b}\, x\, \rho(x)\, dx = \frac{1}{b-a}\,\int_a^b x dx = \frac{1}{b-a}\left( b^2 - a^2\right) = \frac{b + a}{2}. \end{equation*}
    The variance, which is square of the standard deviation \(\sigma\text{,}\) is similarly calculated to yield
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \left(\mathbb{E}[X]\right)^2 = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}, \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int_a^b x^2\,\rho(x)\, dx = \frac{1}{3}\left(b^2 + ba + a^2 \right). \end{equation*}
    Thus, standard deviation \(\sigma\) is just the square root of the variance, as usual.
    \begin{equation*} \sigma = \frac{|b-a|}{2\sqrt{3}}. \end{equation*}
    The cumulative distribution function, \(F(x)\text{,}\) which gives the probability that \(X \le x\) is easily calculated for uniform distribution.
    \begin{equation} F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x\, \rho(x')\,dx' \equiv \text{ Probability that } X \lt x,\tag{1.8.2} \end{equation}
    where I have used \(x'\) for the dummy variable since \(x\) now is a particular value. This will be a step function since the formula resulting from the integration depends on where the point \(x\) happens to lie.
    \begin{equation} F(x) = \begin{cases} 0 \, \amp \text{if } x \lt a \\ \frac{x-a}{b-a} \amp \text{if } a \le x \le b \\ 1 \amp \text{if } x \gt b. \end{cases}\tag{1.8.3} \end{equation}
    The last line says that probability that \(X\) to have less than any value in \((b,\infty)\) is 1.0 since obviously the entire non-zero value range, viz., \([a,b]\text{,}\) is included in this case. The second line says that the probability increases linearly between \(a\) and \(b\) since the PDF is constant in this range.
    Figure 1.8.1 shows plots of PDF and CDF of uniform distribution \(U(0,10)\text{.}\) You can see that as we scan through the interval of the uniform PDF, the probability accumulates in the CDF and eventually, CDF becomes \(1\text{,}\) which represents probability of any of the values in the interval.
    To generate these plots import: from scipy.stats import uniform and then use the methods uniform.pdf() and uniform.cdf().

    Subsubsection 1.8.1.1 Inverse Uniform CDF

    Since the CDF of uniform random variable is linear in the range of interest, the inverse is also linear and turns out to be trivial. Recall the CDF of \(U(a,b)\) in the range \([a,b]\) is
    \begin{equation*} F(x) = -\frac{a}{b-a} + \frac{x}{b-a}\quad a\le x \le b, \end{equation*}
    which is linear in its argument \(x\text{.}\) The inverse will be
    \begin{equation*} F^{-1}(p) = a + (b-a) p, \end{equation*}
    which is also linear in its argument \(p\text{.}\) Let’s check if that’s true.
    \begin{equation*} F^{-1}(\ F(x)\ ) = a + (b-a) F(x) = a + (b-a) \frac{x - a}{b - a} = x. \end{equation*}
    So, if you wanted to generate samples in the range \(a \le x \le b\) that act like they are sampled from the uniform distribution \(U(a,b)\text{,}\) you would first generate pseudo-random numbers in unit interval \([0,1]\) using algorithms lke Mersenne Twister. Suppose, you have \(100\) such sample \(p_1, p_2, \cdots, p_{100}\text{.}\) Then, you will plug them in the inverse CDF \(F^{-1}\) to generate samples \(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_{100}\) from \(U(a,b)\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} x_i = F^{-1}(p_i) = a + (b-a) p_i. \end{equation*}
    This is trivial here since we have an analytic expression for the inverse CDF. In other distributions, such as the Gaussian/Normal distribution, the inverse can be computed only numerically. The stats packages usually have functions that do it for you. For instance, ppf() method in Python/scipy.stats is used for that purpose. In case of uniform distribution, the command is
    from scipy.stats import uniform
samples = uniform.ppf(u, loc=a, scale=b-a).

    Subsection 1.8.2 Normal (Gaussian) Distribution

    The PDF of a Gaussian or Normal distribution is a bell-shaped curve with only two parameters, a mean \(\mu\) and a standard deviation \(\sigma\text{.}\) The name Gaussian is preferred in Physics and Engineering, and Normal is preferred in statistics and data science. In these notes I will use both of them, just for fun.
    The PDF of the Gaussian distribution of mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) for a scalar variable \(X\) is defined by
    \begin{equation} \rho(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}}\,\exp\left( - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right),\tag{1.8.4} \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation*} \exp(a) = e^a. \end{equation*}
    The reason I am writing \(\exp(a)\) rather than \(e^a\) is that exponent in the latter expression usually prints too small on the screen. While doing calculations by hand, you should stick to \(e^a\) notation.
    When the distribution of a random variable \(X\) is Gaussian of mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\text{,}\) i.e., variance \(\sigma^2\) we denote this as a short hand notation by
    \begin{equation*} X \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mu, \sigma^2\right). \end{equation*}
    The special case of \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\) is called standard normal distribution. A standard normal random variable \(X\) will obey
    \begin{equation*} X \sim \mathcal{N}\left( 0, 1\right). \end{equation*}
    As always, the PDF in Eq. (1.8.4) has the probability interpretation in an infinitesimal interval around \(x\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} P(x \le X \le x + dx) = \rho(x)\, dx. \end{equation*}
    Thus, if you want the probability of \(X \in [-1, 3]\text{,}\) you will just integrate it.
    \begin{equation*} P(X \in [-1, 3]) = \int_{-1}^3\, \rho(x) dx = \int_{-1}^3\, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}}\,\exp\left( - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\, dx. \end{equation*}
    The CDF \(F(x)\) is just such an integral for the probability of \(X \in (-\infty, x]\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x\, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}}\,\exp\left( - \frac{(x'-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\, dx'.\tag{1.8.5} \end{equation}
    The integral is unwieldy and only done numerically except when \(x=\infty\text{.}\) If \(x=\infty\text{,}\) then entire real line is included. That would make
    \begin{equation*} F(\infty) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}}\,\exp\left( - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\, dx = 1. \end{equation*}
    While the CDF of a general Gaussian is denoted by \(F(x)\text{,}\) the CDF of Standard Normal, i.e., \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) is given it’s own symbol, \(\Phi(x)\) and it’s inverse by \(\Phi^{-1}(p)\text{.}\)
    Figure 1.8.2 shows plots of PDF and CDF of Gaussian distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{.}\) Note the bell-shape of the PDF and the soft step of the CDF which goes from \(0\) to \(1\) monotonically. To generate these plots import: from scipy.stats import uniform and then use the methods norm.pdf() and norm.cdf().
    The mean and variance of a Gaussian is in the definition itself and can be readily checked if you know how do Gaussian integrals.
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\, x\, \rho(x)\, dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}}\, \int_{-\infty}^\infty\, x\, \exp\left( - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) dx = \mu. \\ \amp \textrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X]^2 - \left( \mathbb{E}[X]\right)^2 = \sigma^2. \end{align*}

    Remark 1.8.3. Doing Gaussian Integrals.

    Here are couple of tricks of doing Gaussian integrals.
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left( -kx^2\right)\, dx = \sqrt{ \frac{\pi}{k} }\\ \amp\int_{-\infty}^\infty x^{2n+1}\,\exp\left( - kx^2 \right)\, dx = 0,\ \text{for } n = 0, 1,2, \cdots\\ \amp \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^{2n}\,\exp\left( - kx^2 \right)\, dx = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left( -\frac{d}{dk}\right)^n\,\exp\left( - kx^2 \right)\, dx \\ \amp\qquad = \left( -\frac{d}{dk}\right)^n \, \int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left( -kx^2\right)\, dx \\ \amp\qquad = \left( -\frac{d}{dk}\right)^n\,\sqrt{ \frac{\pi}{k} } = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4^n}\,\frac{(2n)!}{n!}\frac{1}{k^{n + \frac{1}{2}}}. \end{align*}

    Subsubsection 1.8.2.1 Inverse CDF of Gaussian and Sampling

    Sampling using inverse Gaussian CDF is a very common operation in computer simulation and statitical analysis. Just to be sure, recall that CDF is a mapping from \(x\in \mathbb{R}\) to \(p \in [0, 1]\) and the inverse will do the opposite mapping. In the case of the uniform didtribution since CDF was linear, the inverse CDF was also linear.
    However, the CDF of Gaussian is known only as an intractable integral,
    \begin{equation*} F(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2} }\,\int_{-\infty}^x\, \exp\left( -\frac{ (x - \mu)^2 }{2\sigma^2}\right)\, dx, \end{equation*}
    the inverse is computed only numerically. Fortunately, all standard stats packages already have them and it is just a matte of calling a function. In SciPy pacakge, scipy.stats.norm.ppf(p) will produce the \(x\) value corresponding to the probability level \(p\text{.}\)
    Although, general Gaussian distributions are available in the computer libraries, most commonly, it is the standard normal distibution \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) that we use. As has been stated earlier, the CDF for the standard normal is given a special symbol, viz., \(\Phi\) and the inverse by \(\Phi^{-1}\)
    The process of drawing random samples for either \(F\) or \(\Phi\) of Gaussian begins as for any other distribution by first getting hold of uniformly disrtibuted (pseudo)random values \(p \in [0,1]\text{,}\) which are then feed into the ppf() function of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{.}\) The steps are as follows.
    • Generate uniform random numbers \(p_1, p_2, \cdots, p_N\, \sim \, U(0,1)\text{.}\)
    • Apply \(x_i = \mu + \sigma \times \Phi^{-1}(p_i)\text{,}\) if using inverse of standard normal. If using scipy.stats pacakge, the code for \(F^{-1}\) will be:
      scipy.stats.norm.ppf(\(p_i\text{,}\) loc = \(0\text{,}\) scale = \(1\)).
      In scipy.stats, you can include the actual loc and scale in the argument itself as shown in the program listing below. In that case: Apply \(x_i = F^{-1}(p_i)=\text{scipy.stats.norm.ppf}(p_i, \text{loc} = \mu, \text{scale} =\sigma)\text{.}\)
    • Use \(x_i\) as your Gaussian samples.
    Figure 1.8.4 displays an an example of drawing from a Gaussian distribution and how the samples match up with the theoretical distribution. Clearly, the histogram based on the samples is very representative of the theoretical curve. It was produced by the code below.
    import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import norm

# Parameters
mu, sigma = 0, 1 # Gaussian if your choice
n_samples = 1000

# Step 1: uniform samples
u = np.random.rand(n_samples)

# Step 2: transform with inverse CDF (ppf)
samples = norm.ppf(u, loc=mu, scale=sigma)

# Plot histogram vs theoretical PDF
x = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
pdf = norm.pdf(x, mu, sigma)

plt.figure(figsize=(7,5))
plt.hist(samples, bins=30, density=True, alpha=0.6, label="Sampled (inverse CDF)")
plt.plot(x, pdf, 'r-', lw=2, label="Theoretical PDF")
plt.xlabel("x")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.title("Sampling from Normal(0,1) using Inverse CDF")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

    Subsection 1.8.3 Exponential Distribution

    Exponential distribution is commonly used to model the time till first success in a Possion process. Recall that in a Poisson process events occur independently and at a constant average rate, usually denoted by letter \(\lambda\text{.}\) The exponential random variable can take any value on zero and positive real line, \(X \in [0, \infty)\) with the PDF given by
    \begin{equation} \rho(x) = \lambda\,e^{-\lambda\,x}.\tag{1.8.6} \end{equation}
    As usual, it has the following probability interpretation.
    \begin{equation*} P(x \le X \le x+dx) = \rho(x)\, dx = \lambda\,e^{-\lambda\,x}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    You can verify that the PDF in Eq. (1.8.6) is properly normalized to give probability over the entire range f values, i.e, \(0 \le X \lt \infty\) is \(1\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^\infty\, \rho(x)\, dx = \int_{0}^\infty\, \lambda\, e^{-\lambda\,x}\, dx = 1. \end{equation*}
    The Cumulative Distribution Function, (CDF), is the probability for \((0 \le X \le x\text{.}\) Therefore,
    \begin{equation} F(x) = \int_{0}^x\, \lambda\, e^{-\lambda\,x'}\, dx' = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}. \tag{1.8.7} \end{equation}
    Figure 1.8.5 shows the PDF and CDF of the exponential distribution for \(\lambda = 1\text{.}\)
    The complement of the CDF, i.e., the probability that \(X \gt x\) is called survival function (SF).
    \begin{equation} \textrm{SF}(x) \equiv P(X \gt x) = 1 - F(x) = 1 - \left( 1 - e^{-\lambda x} \right) = e^{-\lambda x}. \tag{1.8.8} \end{equation}
    From the survival function, it is possible to prove an important property of exponential distribution: that it is memoryless, meaning that the probability of an event occurring in the next time interval does not depend on how much time has already elapsed. In formulas, this will be a condition on the conditional probability.
    \begin{equation} P(X \gt (t + s) \mid X \gt s) = P(X \gt t).\tag{1.8.9} \end{equation}
    That is, whether “you wait upto \(X=s\) and then look at the next \(t\) units of time” or “you don’t wait and look at the next \(t\) interval of time”, the two events will give the same probability. That is, the probability for \(t\) intervals of time will be independent of the starting instant.

    Proof of Memoryless Property.

    Let’s write the left side of Eq. (1.8.9) in terms of joint and prior, based on the definition of conditional probabilities given in an earlier section.
    \begin{equation*} P(X \gt t + s \mid X \gt s) = \frac{P( (X \gt t + s) \text{ AND } (X \gt s) )}{ P(X \gt s) }. \end{equation*}
    Since \((t+s \ge s)\) the joint probability, you see that joint probability will simply equal \(P(X \gt t + s)\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} P( (X \gt t + s) \text{ AND } (X \gt s) ) = P(X \gt t + s). \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} P(X \gt t + s \mid X \gt s) = \frac{P(X \gt t + s) }{ P(X \gt s) }. \end{equation*}
    Now, we use the survival function given in Eq. (1.8.8) to write the right hand side quantities and then simplify.
    \begin{equation*} P(X \gt t + s \mid X \gt s) = \frac{\exp(-\lambda(t + s))}{\exp(-\lambda s)} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(X \gt t).\quad \blacksquare \end{equation*}
    Finally, let’s go over the mean and variance of exponential distribution. Mean as usual is the expectation value of the random variable \(X\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \text{Mean}(X) \equiv \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^\infty\, x\,\lambda\,e^{-\lambda x}\,dx = \frac{1}{\lambda}. \end{equation*}
    The variance will be
    \begin{equation*} \textrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \left(\mathbb{E}[X]\right)^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}, \end{equation*}
    from which we get the standard deviation \(\sigma\text{,}\) which is the positive square root of variance.
    \begin{equation*} \sigma = \sqrt{ \textrm{Var}(X) } = \frac{1}{\lambda}. \end{equation*}
    Real-World Examples:
    Radioactive Decay:
    Time until the next decay event for a particle with decay rate \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    Queueing Systems:
    Time until the next customer arrives at a store, assuming arrivals follow a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda = 2\) customers per hour. Mean waiting time = \(0.5\) hours.
    Reliability:
    Lifetime of a lightbulb that fails at a constant rate \(λ=0.001\) failures per hour. The probability it lasts more than \(1000\) hours is
    \begin{equation*} 1 - F(1000; 0.001) = e^{-0.001 \times 1000} = e^{-1} \approx 0.3679\text{.} \end{equation*}

    Subsection 1.8.4 Gamma Distribution

    The exponential distribution gave us distribution of interval till first success in a Poisson process with an average rate of event occuring is given by a parameter \(\lambda\text{.}\) What will be the distribution of intervals till the \(k^\text{th}\) success? Let us call this event by name \(T\) and use letter \(t\) for its values. Then the PDF of the disrtribution of \(T\) is given by a Gamma distribution given by
    \begin{equation} \rho_k(t) = \frac{\lambda^{k}\, t^{k-1}\, e^{-\lambda x}}{(k-1)!},\quad k \ge 1,\tag{1.8.10} \end{equation}
    where I have chosen to include the information that it is the \(k^\text{th}\) we are looking at. Often, in place of rate parameter \(\lambda\text{,}\) we use it’s inverse, called shape parameter:
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \frac{1}{\lambda}. \end{equation*}
    Let’s see how we can derive the expression for the PDF.

    Derivation.

    It turns out that it is far easier to just derive the CDF of this distribution \(F_k(t)\) which will give the probability of \(k^{\text{th}}\) event occuring in less time than \(t\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} F_k(t) = P( T \le t ). \end{equation*}
    But, this probability os complement of event that \((T \gt t)\text{,}\) i.e., in which less than \(k\) events occur in the interval \([0,t]\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} P( T \gt t ) = P(\text{less than } k \text{ events occur during} [0,t]). \end{equation*}
    That is,
    \begin{equation} F_k(t) = 1 - P(\text{less than } k \text{ events occur during} [0,t]).\tag{1.8.11} \end{equation}
    Now, less than \(k\) means, we could have \(0, 1, 2, \cdots (k-1)\) events during \([0,t]\text{,}\) which is easy to work out by using Poisson’s distribution of counting events. Thus,
    \begin{equation*} P( T \gt t ) = \sum_{j = 0}^{k-1}\,\frac{ (\lambda t)^{j} }{j!}\,e^{-\lambda t}. \end{equation*}
    Use this in Eq. (1.8.11) and take derivative to get the PDF.
    \begin{align*} \rho_k(x) \amp = \frac{dF_k}{dx} = -\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\, \frac{j \lambda^j t^{j-1} e^{-\lambda t}}{j!} + \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\, \frac{ \lambda^{j+1} t^{j} e^{-\lambda t}}{j!} \\ \amp = -\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\, \frac{ (\lambda t)^{j-1} e^{-\lambda t}}{(j-1)!} + -\lambda e^{-\lambda x} \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\, \frac{ (\lambda t)^{j} e^{-\lambda t}}{j!} \end{align*}
    Now, we change the summation dummy index in the first sum. Let \(i = j - 1\text{,}\) then \(i\) goes from \(0\) and \(k-2\text{.}\) Then, this sum cancels out all the terms of the second term except the last one.
    \begin{align*} \rho_k(x) \amp = -\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\, \frac{ (\lambda t)^{i} e^{-\lambda t}}{(i)!} + -\lambda e^{-\lambda x} \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\, \frac{ (\lambda t)^{j} e^{-\lambda t}}{j!} \\ \amp = \frac{\lambda^{k} t^{k-1} e^{-\lambda t} }{(k-1)!}. \end{align*}

    Remark 1.8.8. Why is it called Gamma Distribution?

    The name has to do with the factorial in the denominator of the PDF. I will now show you how. In mathematics, there is a special function called, the Gamma function, which is defined by the following integral.
    \begin{equation} \Gamma(k) = \int_0^{\infty}\, x^{k-1}\,e^{-x}\, dx.\tag{1.8.12} \end{equation}
    By doing this integral once using integration by parts, you can show that
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma(k) = (k-1)\, \Gamma(k-1). \end{equation*}
    If we know \(\Gamma(1)\) we will know all the rest.
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma(1) = \int_0^{\infty}\,e^{-x}\, dx = 1. \end{equation*}
    Hence,
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma(k) = (k-1).(k-2).(k-3)\cdots 3.2.1 = (k-1)!\, k\ge 1. \end{equation*}
    Now, we write the Gamma distribution function as,
    \begin{equation*} \rho_k(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(k)}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}\, (\lambda t)^{k-1}\,e^{-\lambda t}. \end{equation*}
    Now, let’s show that this is properly normalized. Since \(t \in [0,\infty)\text{,}\) we have the following integral.
    \begin{align*} \int_0^{\infty}\rho_k(t) dt \amp = \frac{1}{\Gamma(k)} \int_0^{\infty}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}(\lambda t)^{k-1}\,e^{-\lambda t}\, dt \\ \amp = \frac{1}{\Gamma(k)} \int_0^{\infty}\,x^{k-1}\,e^{-x}\, dx\quad (\text{with } x = \lambda t) \\ \amp = \frac{1}{\Gamma(k)} \times \Gamma(k) = 1. \end{align*}
    Now, you see how Gamma function came out naturally in working with the Gamma distribution function. You might say, it’s the normalization constant that you need for making the probability come out right.
    By doing the same type of integral as illustrated in showing the normalization calculation above, you can immediately prove that mean and variance of Gamma distribution are as follows.
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbb{E}[T] = \int_0^\infty\, t\; \rho_k(t) dt = \frac{k}{\lambda}. \\ \amp \textrm{Var}(T) = \mathbb{E}[T^2] -\left( \mathbb{E}[T]\right)^2 = \int_0^\infty\, t^2\; \rho_k(t) dt - \left(\frac{k}{\lambda} \right)^2= \frac{k}{\lambda^2}. \end{align*}
    CDF of Gamma Distribution can be written only as an integral and evaluated numerically for each \(t\text{.}\) The integral part is called incomplete gamma and denoted by lower case gamma \(\gamma\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} F_k(t) = \int_0^t\,\rho_k(u)\, du = \frac{1}{\Gamma(k)}\, \gamma(k,\lambda t),\tag{1.8.13} \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation*} \gamma(k,\lambda t) = \int_0^{\lambda t}\,u^{k-1}\,e^{-u}\, du. \end{equation*}
    In Python’s scipy.special library, there is function \(\textrm{gammainc}\) that actually calculates \(\gamma(k,u)/\Gamma(k)\text{,}\) i.e, the CDF directly.

    Example 1.8.9. Waiting Time for the Third Help Desk Call.

    Suppose that calls arrive at a help desk according to a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda = 4\) calls per hour. In this example we will compute answers to:
    1. Probability that all three calls arrive within 30 minutes.
    2. Probability that it takes more than one hour to receive three calls.
    Let \(T\) be the random variable for the time for the \(3^\text{rd}\) call arrival. Then, from the discussion in this section, we conclude that \(T\) will follow the following Gamma distribution.
    \begin{equation*} T \sim \textrm{Gamma}\left(k=3, \lambda=4\,\text{hr}^{-1} \right). \end{equation*}
    (a) The probability that all 3 calls arrive within \(t = 30\,\text{min} = 0.5\,\text{hr}\) means we are just directly looking for the CDF for this value of \(t\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} F_{k=3}(0.5) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(3)}\,\gamma(k=3, \lambda t = 2). \end{equation*}
    We look this us in Python by \(\textrm{scipy.special.gammainc}(3,2) \text{,}\) which is \(\approx 0.323\text{.}\)
    (b) Probability that it takes more than one hour to receive three calls means the third call must be so that \(T \gt 1\text{ hr}\text{.}\) This is complement of the CDF.
    \begin{align*} P( T \gt 1\text{ hr} ) \amp = 1 - F_{k=3}(t=1.0)\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\Gamma(3)}\,\gamma(k=3, \lambda t = 4) \approx 1- 0.762 = 0.238. \end{align*}
