    Section 1.11 Point Estimates and Confidence Intervals

    Statistics is a useful tool when you are dealing with uncertainty in systems that are too vast to examine every item. In these cases we can collect data on a random subset and use statisical methods to draw and support conclusions that can be reasonably drawn from the data.
    One type of question that shows up in the inference are:
    1. Parameter Estimation to answer the question: "What is the value of the parameter, e.g., mean \(\mu\) or standard deviation \(\sigma\text{?}\) You can estimate not only the means and variances but other parameters as well. Statistical methods also address the question of how good are our estimates - how far off the actual value may be from the estimate and how confident are we in such claims.
    2. Hypothesis Testing to answer questions such as : "Is the value of the parameter exactly such an such?" For example, "Is the average height of an adult human being exactly 5 feet and 4 inches?" and "Is average height of Indians greater than the average height of Chinese?" You can set up hypothesis testing not just of parameters but of pretty much anything, including ML models - "does this model perform better than the other?"".

    Subsection 1.11.1 Parameter Estimation

    Subsubsection 1.11.1.1 Basic Setup

    Let’s recall the basic notation here again. Suppose we have a popolation from which we have collected \(n\) samples. Each sampling process itself is a random variable. For instance, if you toss a coin 3 times, you might get HTT; now, your repeat might give you THT, etc. So, the very first toss is giving you H and T in a random way. That is how we can say that if I conduct \(n\) tosses, each of those tosses will itself be a random variable.
    So, suppose, we have “performed” \(n\) random experiments, each represented by its own random variable. Let’s denote them as before by
    \begin{equation} X_1, X_2, X_3, \cdots, X_n.\tag{1.11.1} \end{equation}
    We define a sample mean, which is just an average of the \(X_i\)’s:
    \begin{equation} \overline{X} = \frac{X_1 + X_2+ X_3+ \cdots+ X_n}{n} \equiv \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\tag{1.11.2} \end{equation}
    and a sample variance
    \begin{equation} S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( X_i - \overline{X} \right)^2\quad (n \gt 1).\tag{1.11.3} \end{equation}
    In sample variance, we devide by \(n-1\) and not \(n\) because the definition of sample mean \(\overline{x}\) has used up one of the degrees of freedom and we are left with one less. This also makes \(S^2\) an unbiased estimator of population variance as we will see below.
    The population mean \(\mu\) and population variance \(\sigma^2\) are of course, unknown. We will see below how to use the sample mean and sample variance to get an estimate of these population quantities.
    Note that both \(\overline{X}\) and \(S^2\) are random variables. So, they have their own probability distributions:
    \begin{equation*} \rho_{\overline{X}}(x),\quad \rho_{S^2}(s^2), \end{equation*}
    where the subscript denotes the random variable and the the small letter inside the braces denotes the value of the variable for the PDF. If we have a discrete variable, we use PMF in place of PDF. Sometimes, we also deal with the random variable \(S\text{,}\) the sample standard deviation,
    \begin{equation} S = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( X_i - \overline{X} \right)^2}\quad (n \gt 1).\tag{1.11.4} \end{equation}
    If the variables \(X_i\)’s take only values \(0\) or \(1\text{,}\) i.e., each experiment is an identical Bernoulli trial, then the sample mean \(\overline{X}\) is the proportion of \(1\)’s in the sample.
    \begin{equation} \text{Sample Proportion},\ \overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\quad X_i\sim \text{Bernoulli}(p),\tag{1.11.5} \end{equation}
    where \(p\) is the probability of success (i.e., \(1\)) in each Bernoulli trial. That is, in this case, sample mean is an estimator of proportion of \(1\)’s in the population.
    Now, we will discuss couple of different ways to estimate population parameters based on the data.

    Subsubsection 1.11.1.2 Maximum Likelihood Estimate

    Let’s start with an example of estimating the probability \(p\) of getting a Heads (\(X=1\)) in a toss of a given coin. We toss it \(n\) times and note the outcomes, \(\{THHT \cdots HTT\}\text{.}\) That’s our data with \(x_1 = T,\ x_2 = H, \cdots x_n=T\text{.}\) Each time we toss, we are doing a Bernoulli trial for which we have PMF:
    \begin{equation} P_X(x) = p^x (1-p)^x.\tag{1.11.6} \end{equation}
    Now, imagine repeating your \(n\) datapoint experiment many times. Each time you will most likely get different data, Say, the data above was \(\{x_1^{(1)}, x_2^{(1)}, \cdots, x_n^{(1)} \}\text{,}\) but it may be some other sequence of \(T, H\text{,}\) say, \(\{x_1^{(2)}, x_2^{(2)}, \cdots, x_n^{(2)} \}\) the seocnd time, etc. Presumably, you are actually conducting experiment in an \(n\)-dimensional space of \((X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n)\) and each collection of data is just one point in this joint space.
    The true data points in this \(n\)-dimensional space maybe concentrated in some region more so than in some other areas; in general, we just do not know. The maximum likelihood estimate is based on assuming that the data you actually got has the highest probability in this \(n\)-dimensional space, i.e., you are most liley to sample the areas where the actual data points are most concentrated in the \((X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n)\) space.
    Since we are dealing with discrete variables, here we work with PMF. Let \(P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n)\) represent the joint PMF of \((X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n)\text{.}\) Since, \(X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n\) are independent random variables, the joint PMF will factorize.
    \begin{equation*} P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n) = P_X(x_1) P_X(x_2)\cdots P_X(x_n) \end{equation*}
    It is convenient to introduce a product symbol,
    \begin{equation*} \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i)\equiv P_X(x_1) P_X(x_2)\cdots P_X(x_n). \end{equation*}
    and write the earlier equation as
    \begin{equation} P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n) = \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i)\tag{1.11.7} \end{equation}
    We know expression of the PMF of the individual trials as given in Eq. (1.11.6) since we are doing Bernoulli trials here. If we were doing some other experiements, we would have a different expression for them with their own parameters. Thus,
    \begin{align*} P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n)\amp = \Pi_{i=1}^n p^{x_i} (1-p)^{1-x_i}\\ \amp = p^{\sum_i x_i} (1-p)^{\sum_i(1-x_i)}. \end{align*}
    This gives us an expression of the joint probability in terms of the data, i.e., \(\{x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n \}\) and the unknown parameter\(p\text{.}\) In this context we treat \(P_J\) as a function of the parameter \(p\) and call it likelihood function, denoted by \(L\)
    \begin{equation} L = B_J.\tag{1.11.8} \end{equation}
    Thus, we have the following function that we need to maximize with respect to the paremeter \(p\) to obtain the maximum likelihood estimate of \(p\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} L(p) = p^{\sum_i x_i} (1-p)^{\sum_i(1-x_i)}. \end{equation*}
    In this instance, taking derivative with respect to \(p\) is not that hard, but in general, we maximize the log of \(L\text{.}\) Since log is a monotonic function, the maximum of a log of a function will occur at the same place as the function itself. The log likelihood, denoted by \(LL\)
    \begin{equation*} LL (p) \equiv \log ( L (p) ) = \left(\sum_i x_i \right) \log p + \left( \sum_i(1-x_i) \right) \log (1-p). \end{equation*}
    Taking derivative with respect to \(p\) and setting \(p=p_\text{MLE}\text{,}\) we can solve for \(p_\text{MLE}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d LL(p)}{dp} = \frac{\sum_i x_i}{p_\text{MLE}} - \frac{\sum_i(1-x_i)}{1-p_\text{MLE}}. \end{equation*}
    Noting that \(\sum_i 1 = n\) here, we get
    \begin{equation} p_\text{MLE} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i }{n}.\tag{1.11.9} \end{equation}
    This is just the average of the collected data on heads and tails, with heads being \(1\) and tails being \(0\text{.}\)
    Remark 1.11.1. What if \(X\) was not Bernoulli?
    We just have to proceed a little differently after Eq. (1.11.7). In that equation, parameters, usually denoted by \(\theta\text{,}\) of interest will appear in the probability distributions of the individual trials, same for every one of them.
    \begin{equation} P_J(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n) = \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i;\theta).\tag{1.11.10} \end{equation}
    Then, the likelihood function will just be
    \begin{equation} L(\theta) = \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i;\theta).\tag{1.11.11} \end{equation}
    The loglikelihood
    \begin{equation} LL(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \left( P_X(x_i;\theta)\right).\tag{1.11.12} \end{equation}
    In general, setting the derivative of \(LL\) to zero and solving for the MLE estimate can only be done numercally.
    Example 1.11.2. Each Trial \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) of unknown \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\).
    We have
    \begin{equation*} P_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}\, e^{ -(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2 }}. \end{equation*}
    In this case, suppose we want an MLE of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\text{.}\) The log likelihood function will be (replacing \(\theta\) by the actual parameters here in Eq. (1.11.12).)
    \begin{align*} LL(\mu, \sigma^2) \amp \sum_{i=1}^n \log \left( P_X(x_i;\theta)\right). \\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^n\left[ - \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi \sigma^2) - \frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right]. \end{align*}
    Now, to find \(\mu_\text{MLE}\) and \(\sigma_\text{MLE}\) you will solve the following equations.
    \begin{align*} \amp \left. \frac{\partial LL(\mu, \sigma^2)}{\partial \mu} \right|_{\mu_\text{MLE}, \sigma^2_\text{MLE}} = 0 \\ \amp \left. \frac{\partial LL(\mu, \sigma)}{\partial \sigma^2} \right|_{\mu_\text{MLE}, \sigma^2_\text{MLE}} = 0 \end{align*}
    Show that you get the following answers.
    \begin{align*} \amp \mu_\text{MLE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n x_i \equiv \bar x,\\ \amp \sigma_\text{MLE}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n \left(x_i - \bar x \right)^2 \end{align*}
    Definition 1.11.3. Estimators.
    From the formulas for MLE etimates of \(p\) of a Bernoulli \(X\) and \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) of a Gaussian \(X\text{,}\) we can write random variables whose expectation values estimate these quantities. We call these random variables estimators. We will denote them with capital letters as we have done for other random variables.
    Thus the MLE estimator of \(p\) of a Bernoulli variable \(X\) will be
    \begin{equation} \Pi_\text{MLE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i, \text{ same as }\overline{X}.\tag{1.11.13} \end{equation}
    Similarly, the MLE estimators of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) for a Gaussian/Normal variable \(X\) will be
    \begin{align} \amp M_\text{MLE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i, \text{ same as }\overline{X},\tag{1.11.14}\\ \amp \Sigma_\text{MLE}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n \left(X_i - M \right)^2\tag{1.11.15} \end{align}
    If expectation values of these estimators in their corresponding probability distributions yield the appropriate true values, \(p\) for the Bernoulli case, and \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) for the Normal case, we say that the estimator is an unbiased estimartor.
    Bernoulli \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\) estimator is unbiased..
    To prove that \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\) is unbiased, we calculate it/s expectation value in the Bernoulli PMF. We know that for expectation value of each \(X_i\) is same:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X_i] = p. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\left[ \Pi_\text{MLE} \right] \amp = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}\left[ X_i \right] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} p = \frac{1}{n} \times n\,p = p. \end{align*}
    Hence \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\) is an unbiased estimator of \(p\text{.}\) \(\quad \blacksquare\)
    Gaussian \(M_\text{MLE}\) estimator is unbiased..
    Here, we know that \(\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu\) for each \(X_i\text{.}\) Hence, just like the Bernoulli \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\text{,}\) the expectation value of \(M_\text{MLE}\) is also unbiased.
    Gaussian \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\) estimator is not unbiased..
    Now, we come to an estimator which is not unbiased. Let’s see. Since each \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\text{,}\) the variance of each \(X_i\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] - \left( \mathbb{E}[X_i] \right)^2 = \sigma^2 \implies \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\tag{1.11.16} \end{equation}
    When we expand the parenthesis in Eq. (1.11.15), we will find that we also need:
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[M^2] = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2\tag{1.11.17} \end{equation}
    Here is how I got this result.
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[M^2] \amp = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n X_i X_j\right]\, \quad\text{Use two indices, one for each }M.\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2}\mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=j} X_i X_j + \sum_{i\ne j} X_i X_j \right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\left[X_i^2\right] + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i\ne j} \mathbb{E}\left[X_i X_j\right] \\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 \right) + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i\ne j} \mu\times \mu,\ (\text{ since }X_i \text{ and } X_{j\ne i}\text{ independent.})\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2} \left(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 \right) \sum_{i=1}^n 1 + \frac{1}{n^2} \mu^2 \sum_{i\ne j} 1.\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n} \left(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 \right) + \frac{n(n-1)}{n^2} \mu^2,\quad \text{ since} \sum_{i\ne j} 1 = n(n-1). \\ \amp = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2. \end{align*}
    Another term that will show up in our calculation is
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[MX_i] = \sigma^2 + n\,\mu^2. \end{equation*}
    I leave this part to you to complete. Now, we look at the expectation of the estimator \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\left[ \Sigma_\text{MLE}^2 \right] \amp = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n \left(X_i - M \right)^2 \right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1^n} \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] - \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[MX_i] + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[M^2] \\ \amp = \frac{1}{n}\times n\, \left( \sigma^2 + \mu^2\right) - \frac{2}{n} \left( \sigma^2 + n\,\mu^2 \right) + \frac{1}{n}\times n \left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2 \right)\\ \amp = \frac{n-1}{n}\,\sigma^2 \ne \sigma^2 \end{align*}
    Hence \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\) is not an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\) of a Normal variable. \(\qquad \blacksquare\)
    From the result above, it is immediately obvious that had we divided by \((n-)\) in the definition of \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\) rather than by \(n\text{,}\) the modified \(\Sigma_\text{uMLE}^2\) will be an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} \Sigma_\text{uMLE}^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( X_i - M \right)^2.\tag{1.11.18} \end{equation}
    You can verify that expectation value of this estimator will be \(\sigma^2\text{.}\) Interestingly, although \(\Sigma_\text{uMLE}^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\text{,}\) its square root \(\Sigma_\text{uMLE}\) is not an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    A less algebraic way of proving the unbiasedness of \(\Sigma_\text{uMLE}^2\) is to recall the chi square disrtribution variable. A Gaussian variable \(X\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and sample variance defined by \(S^2\) defined by
    \begin{equation*} S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i \left( X_i - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_i \right)^2. \end{equation*}
    Then, the following is true
    \begin{equation*} Y \equiv \frac{(n-1) S^2 }{\sigma^2} \sim \chi_{n-1}^2. \end{equation*}
    The expectation value of the \(\chi^2\) variable is the degree of freedom of the \(\chi^2\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[Y] = n-1, \end{equation*}
    which is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{(n-1) S^2 }{\sigma^2} \right] = n-1. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[S^2] = \sigma^2. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 1.11.2 Confidence Interval TODO

    From the point estimation section above, we learned how to use maximum likelihood procedure on the sampled data for computing parameters that model distribution of a random variable in nature. For instance, we saw that sample mean gives an unbiased estimate of the probability of success (\(1\)’s) in a \(0/1\) system. But, how can we be sure that the estimated value is close to the true value since the later is not known? This is where the idea of confidence interval comes in.

    Definition 1.11.4.

    A confidence interval for a parameter \(\theta\) is an interval of values, \((\theta_\text{low}, \theta_\text{high})\) which contains the true unknown (maybe, even unknowable) value of \(\theta\) at a particular level of confidence. The confidence level is usually written as a percentage using a complementary quantity \(\alpha\) as
    \begin{equation*} \text{Confidence Level } = (1-\alpha)100\%, \end{equation*}
    where \(\alpha\) is a fraction. The fractional quantity \((1-\alpha)\) is called confidence coefficient. For instance,, if we want \(95\%\) confidence level, we would have
    \begin{equation*} 95\%\,\text{Confidence Level} \iff \alpha = 1- 0.95 = 0.05. \end{equation*}
    Clearly, greater the confidence level (or, equivalently, smaller \(\alpha\)), greater should be our confidence that the unknown parameter is contained somewhere inside the confidence interval.
    Thus, we define confidence interval \((\theta_\text{low}, \theta_\text{high})\) by requiring that the following probability statement be true.
    \begin{equation*} P(\theta_\text{low} \lt \theta \lt \theta_\text{high}) = (1-\alpha). \end{equation*}
    We will see examples of how we can compute \(\theta_\text{low}\) and \(\theta_\text{high}\) from the data collected and some assumptions about the distribution from which data may have arisen.

    Subsubsection 1.11.2.1 The Z Intervals

    The confidence interval, called the Z interval, is a confidence interval of the population mean \(\mu\) when we are provided with the exact value of the population variance \(\sigma^2\text{.}\) At this point, let’s not worry about how we somehow know the population variance but not the population mean. We are assuming this so that we can apply the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) to this problem. Out aim is to lay out the theoretical steps in an understandable way so when we remove the assumption about the sample variance, it will not be too high a barrier. The procedure is best illustrated by a numerical example.
    Supose our project is to study height of male adults in a populatoin in which the height varies with a known variance of \(\sigma^2 = 25\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\) We wish to find the confidence interval of the mean height \(\mu\) at the confidence level of \(95\%\text{,}\) i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} \alpha = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05. \end{equation*}
    We collect data by measuring the heights of \(50\) randomly selected adults. From which, we can of course find the sample mean. Say, we found the result \(170 \, \text{cm}\text{.}\) As before, we use capital letters for the random variable and small letters for their values.
    \begin{equation*} \bar{h} \equiv \overline{H} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nh_i = 170\, \text{cm}. \end{equation*}
    So, we have \(\bar{h} = 170\, \text{cm}\) from sample and \(\sigma = 5\,\text{cm}\) from the population. So, how do we get a confidence interval for \(\mu\text{?}\)
    Recall that according to CLT, if a variable \(H\) has population mean \(\mu\text{,}\) population variance \(\sigma^2\text{,}\) and \(n\) points of data, \(H_1, H_2, \cdots, H_n\text{,}\) are collected then, for large enough \(n\text{,}\) the following variable has a standard normal distribution.
    \begin{equation} Z \equiv \frac{\overline{H} - \mu}{ \sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),\tag{1.11.19} \end{equation}
    where \(\overline{H}\) is the sample mean. Supposing that \(n=50\) is large enough to apply CLT to our height problem, we can this \(Z\) variabale to connect to the probability statement about the confidence interval to find the low and high values of the heights.
    \begin{equation} P\left( z_\text{low} \lt Z \lt z_\text{high} \right) = 1-\alpha = 0.95.\tag{1.11.20} \end{equation}
    What are these \(z_\text{low}\) and \(z_\text{high}\text{?}\) To find these values, we look at the PDF and CDF of a standard Normal disribution as shown in Figure 1.11.5. The CDF shows that to include the middle \((1-\alpha)\) of the probability, we need the following values of \(Z\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z_\text{low} = - z_{\alpha/2}, \quad z_\text{high} = z_{\alpha/2}. \end{equation*}
    The value of \(z_{\alpha/2}\) corresponds to the probability value
    \begin{equation*} p_{\alpha/2} = 1-\frac{\alpha}{2} = 1 - \frac{0.05}{2} = 0.975. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, we solve the following equation to get \(z_{high}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} \Phi(z_\text{high}) = p_{\alpha/2}.\tag{1.11.21} \end{equation}
    Inverting this equation gives
    \begin{equation} z_\text{high} \equiv z_{\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}( p_{\alpha/2} ) = \Phi^{-1}( 0.975 ) \approx 1.96.\tag{1.11.22} \end{equation}
    This solved our immediate problem of \(z_\text{low}\) and \(z_\text{high}\text{.}\)
    Having found that \(z_\text{low}\) and \(z_\text{high}\) are just \(-z_{\alpha/2}\) and \(z_{\alpha/2}\text{,}\) let us rewrite Eq.(1.11.20) as follows. or equivalently,
    \begin{equation} P\left( -z_{\alpha/2} \lt \frac{\overline{H} - \mu}{ \sigma/\sqrt{n}} \lt z_{\alpha/2} \right) = 1-\alpha,\tag{1.11.23} \end{equation}
    The event of this probability can be rewritten to see the confidential interval of the population mean \(\mu\) that is contained in the statement. Follow the following sequence of steps:
    \begin{align*} \amp P\left( -z_{\alpha/2} \lt \frac{\overline{H} - \mu}{ \sigma/\sqrt{n}} \lt z_{\alpha/2} \right) = 1-\alpha\\ \amp P\left( -z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \lt \overline{H} - \mu \lt z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha \\ \amp P\left( +z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \gt \mu - \overline{H} \gt - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha\\ \amp P\left( \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \gt \mu \gt \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha\\ \amp P\left(\overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \lt \mu \lt \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha \end{align*}
    From the statement of the event we can immediately read the low and high limit on the population mean \(\mu\) at the confidence level \((1-\alpha)100\%\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} \left( \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\ \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right)\tag{1.11.24} \end{equation}
    In the present example, we will have the following numerical values.
    \begin{align*} \amp \overline{H} = 170\,\text{cm}\\ \amp z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 1.96\times \frac{5\,\text{cm}}{\sqrt{50}} = 1.39\,\text{ cm}\\ \amp \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} - 1.39\,\text{cm} = 168.61\,\text{cm}\\ \amp \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} + 1.39\,\text{cm} = 171.39\,\text{cm} \end{align*}
    Hence, the \(95\%\) confidence interval for heights of adult males in the population is \((168.61\,\text{cm},\ 171.39\,\text{cm})\text{.}\) This interval is called Z interval because we use the Standard Normal distribution. It is applicable when we use a known population variance. As has already been pointed out that this is not the case in real settings. The variance must also be estimated from the collected data. We turn to remedy this problem in the next section.
    Correct and Wrong Interpretations of Confidence Intervals: How should you interpret the following probability statement?
    \begin{equation*} P\left(\overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \lt \mu \lt \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha \end{equation*}
    To simplify notation in our discussion, let’s rewrite this equation with \(L\) for the lower end and \(U\) for the upper end of the Z-interval.
    \begin{equation} P\left(L \lt \mu \lt U \right) = 1-\alpha,\tag{1.11.25} \end{equation}
    with
    \begin{equation*} L = \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\quad U = \overline{H}+ z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}. \end{equation*}
    Notice that \(\overline{H}\) depends on the data \(\{ h_1, h_2, \cdots, h_{50}\}\) we had collected by measuring the heights of \(50\) adult males chisen randomly from the population. Therefore, \(L\) and \(U\) depend on the data as well. It’s highly likely that if we repeat this experiment of randomly selecting \(50\) adult males and measuring their heights, you will get a different result. Hence, \(L\) and \(U\) are also random variables.
    But population mean \(\mu\) is one definitely number, which would the average of ALL males in the population. Although \(\mu\) is not known but there is no uncertainty in what it is supposed to be. Therefore, we cannot say probability of \(\mu\) to be in this interval is such and such - you can only assign probability to uncertain events and value of \(\mu\) is not uncertain.
    Here, the uncertainty is in the confidence interval since both \(L\) and \(U\) are uncertain, depending on the data instances. As you repeat collection of data, you will get different \((L,U)\) from each data instance. Some of those ranges will include \(\mu\) some will not. The way to interpret Eq. (1.11.25) is that among numerous repeats of data, \((-1\alpha)100%\) of the time the \((L,U)\) range will include \(\mu\text{.}\) Whether a particular interval will include the \(\mu\) is either a yes or no answer, not a probability answer. You might ask about the probability of the event that any one of the confidence interval includes \(\mu\) in it - the answer to that probability will be \((1-\alpha)100%\text{.}\)

    Subsubsection 1.11.2.2 The t-Intervals

    Let’s study the same problem of determining the mean height of a population of male adults in a population. We had collected \(50\) heights, \(\{h_1, h_2, \cdots, h_{50} \}\) and computed their sample mean to be \(\overline{h} = 170 \, \text{cm}\text{.}\) We know nothing about either population mean \(\mu\) or population variance \(\sigma^2\text{.}\) But, in addition to \(\overline{h}\text{,}\) we can compute sample variance from the \(h_i\)’s, e.g., the unbiased sample mean.
    \begin{equation} S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n\left( H_i - \overline{H} \right),\tag{1.11.26} \end{equation}
    where I have written everything in terms of the randdom variables for each sampling step, viz., \(\left(H_1, H_2, \cdots, H_{n}\right)\) with \(n=50\) here, and
    \begin{equation*} \overline{H} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} H_i. \end{equation*}
    Suppose, we found that the value of \(S^2\) from our data, now denoted by the lower case letter, since we are talking about a value of \(S^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} s^2 = 49\, \text{cm}^2 \end{equation*}
    How would we now use \(\overline{h}\) and \(s^2\) to find confidence interval at confidence level \((1-\alpha)100%\text{?}\)
    This is where the theory of Student \(t\) distribution comes handy since the following variable \(T\) obes a \(t\) distribution of degrees of freedom equal to \((n-1)\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} T = \frac{ \overline{H}- \mu }{ S/\sqrt{n} } \sim t_{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    The parallels of \(T\) here and \(Z\) in Eq. (1.11.19) is obvious with only changes being
    \begin{equation*} \sigma, Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \longrightarrow S, T\sim t_{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    Figure 1.11.6 shows parallels to Figure 1.11.5 and illustrates that all we need to do is to use \(t\) distribution of \((n-1) = 50-1 = 49\) degrees of freedom here. The value of \(T\) separating the included exluded excluded probabilities is very close to the corresponding \(Z\) values since we have a fairly high degree of freedom.
    \begin{equation*} t_{n-1, \alpha/2} = t_{50-1, 0.025} \approx 2.01 \text{ compare to } z_{0.025} \approx 1.96. \end{equation*}
    The difference is atrtributable to the flat tail aspect of the \(t\) distribution compared to the standard normal distribution.
    Therefore, the \(t\) confidence interval at confidence interval \((1-\alpha)100%\) is
    \begin{equation} \left(\overline{H}- t_{n-1, \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\ \overline{H} + t_{n-1, \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right) \tag{1.11.27} \end{equation}
    Numerically, in our example, we will get
    \begin{align*} \amp \overline{H} = 170\,\text{cm}\\ \amp t_{n-1, \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = 2.01\times \frac{7\,\text{cm}}{\sqrt{50}} = 1.99\,\text{ cm}\\ \amp \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} - 1.99\,\text{cm} = 168.01\,\text{cm}\\ \amp \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} + 1.99\,\text{cm} = 171.99\,\text{cm} \end{align*}
    That is the \(t\) distribution will be
    \begin{equation*} t\text{-Confidence Interval at } 95\% = \left( 168.01\,\text{cm},\ 171.99\,\text{cm} \right). \end{equation*}
