    Section 1.11 Point Estimates and Confidence Intervals

    Statistics is a useful tool when you are dealing with uncertainty in systems that are too vast to examine every item. In these cases we can collect data on a random subset and use statisical methods to draw and support conclusions that can be reasonably drawn from the data.
    One type of question that shows up in the inference are:
    1. Parameter Estimation to answer the question: "What is the value of the parameter, e.g., mean \(\mu\) or standard deviation \(\sigma\text{?}\) You can estimate not only the means and variances but other parameters as well. Statistical methods also address the question of how good are our estimates - how far off the actual value may be from the estimate and how confident are we in such claims.
    2. Hypothesis Testing to answer questions such as : "Is the value of the parameter exactly such an such?" For example, "Is the average height of an adult human being exactly 5 feet and 4 inches?" and "Is average height of Indians greater than the average height of Chinese?" You can set up hypothesis testing not just of parameters but of pretty much anything, including ML models - "does this model perform better than the other?"".
    Both of these topics deserve their own sections. In this section we will tackle issues associated with the point estimation and defer the hypothesis testing to the next section.

    Subsection 1.11.1 Parameter Estimation

    Subsubsection 1.11.1.1 Basic Setup

    Let’s recall the basic notation here again. Suppose we have a popolation from which we have collected \(n\) samples. Each sampling process itself is a random variable. For instance, if you toss a coin 3 times, you might get HTT; now, your repeat might give you THT, etc. So, the very first toss is giving you H and T in a random way. That is how we can say that if I conduct \(n\) tosses, each of those tosses will itself be a random variable.
    So, suppose, we have “performed” \(n\) random experiments, each represented by its own random variable. Let’s denote them as before by
    \begin{equation} X_1, X_2, X_3, \cdots, X_n.\tag{1.11.1} \end{equation}
    We define a sample mean, which is just an average of the \(X_i\)’s:
    \begin{equation} \overline{X} = \frac{X_1 + X_2+ X_3+ \cdots+ X_n}{n} \equiv \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\tag{1.11.2} \end{equation}
    and a sample variance
    \begin{equation} S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( X_i - \overline{X} \right)^2\quad (n \gt 1).\tag{1.11.3} \end{equation}
    In sample variance, we devide by \(n-1\) and not \(n\) because the definition of sample mean \(\overline{x}\) has used up one of the degrees of freedom and we are left with one less. This also makes \(S^2\) an unbiased estimator of population variance as we will see below.
    The population mean \(\mu\) and population variance \(\sigma^2\) are of course, unknown. We will see below how to use the sample mean and sample variance to get an estimate of these population quantities.
    Note that both \(\overline{X}\) and \(S^2\) are random variables. So, they have their own probability distributions:
    \begin{align*} \amp \overline{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \right),\,\qquad \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1} \end{align*}
    Sometimes, we also deal with the random variable \(S\text{,}\) the sample standard deviation,
    \begin{equation} S = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( X_i - \overline{X} \right)^2}\quad (n \gt 1).\tag{1.11.4} \end{equation}
    If the variables \(X_i\)’s take only values \(0\) or \(1\text{,}\) i.e., each experiment is an identical Bernoulli trial, then the sample mean \(\overline{X}\) is the proportion of \(1\)’s in the sample.
    \begin{equation} \text{Sample Proportion},\ \overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\quad X_i\sim \text{Bernoulli}(p),\tag{1.11.5} \end{equation}
    where \(p\) is the probability of success (i.e., \(1\)) in each Bernoulli trial. That is, in this case, sample mean is an estimator of proportion of \(1\)’s in the population.
    Now, we will discuss a procedure called maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to estimate population parameters based on the data. There is another commonly used method of estimation maximum a posteriori estimation (MAP) based on Bayes’ principle will be discussed in a future section.

    Subsubsection 1.11.1.2 Maximum Likelihood Estimate

    Let’s start with an example of estimating the probability \(p\) of getting a Heads (\(X=1\)) in a toss of a given coin. We toss it \(n\) times and note the outcomes, \(\{THHT \cdots HTT\}\text{.}\) That’s our data with \(x_1 = T,\ x_2 = H, \cdots x_n=T\text{.}\) Each time we toss, we are doing a Bernoulli trial for which we have PMF:
    \begin{equation} P_X(x) = p^x (1-p)^x.\tag{1.11.6} \end{equation}
    Now, imagine repeating your \(n\) datapoint experiment many times. Each time you will most likely get different data, Say, the data above was \(\{x_1^{(1)}, x_2^{(1)}, \cdots, x_n^{(1)} \}\text{,}\) but it may be some other sequence of \(T, H\text{,}\) say, \(\{x_1^{(2)}, x_2^{(2)}, \cdots, x_n^{(2)} \}\) the seocnd time, etc. Presumably, you are actually conducting experiment in an \(n\)-dimensional space of \((X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n)\) and each collection of data is just one point in this joint space.
    The true data points in this \(n\)-dimensional space maybe concentrated in some region more so than in some other areas; in general, we just do not know. The maximum likelihood estimate is based on assuming that the data you actually got has the highest probability in this \(n\)-dimensional space, i.e., you are most liley to sample the areas where the actual data points are most concentrated in the \((X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n)\) space.
    Since we are dealing with discrete variables, here we work with PMF. Let \(P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n)\) represent the joint PMF of \((X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n)\text{.}\) Since, \(X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n\) are independent random variables, the joint PMF will factorize.
    \begin{equation*} P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n) = P_X(x_1) P_X(x_2)\cdots P_X(x_n) \end{equation*}
    It is convenient to introduce a product symbol,
    \begin{equation*} \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i)\equiv P_X(x_1) P_X(x_2)\cdots P_X(x_n). \end{equation*}
    and write the earlier equation as
    \begin{equation} P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n) = \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i)\tag{1.11.7} \end{equation}
    We know expression of the PMF of the individual trials as given in Eq. (1.11.6) since we are doing Bernoulli trials here. If we were doing some other experiements, we would have a different expression for them with their own parameters. Thus,
    \begin{align*} P_{J}(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n)\amp = \Pi_{i=1}^n p^{x_i} (1-p)^{1-x_i}\\ \amp = p^{\sum_i x_i} (1-p)^{\sum_i(1-x_i)}. \end{align*}
    This gives us an expression of the joint probability in terms of the data, i.e., \(\{x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n \}\) and the unknown parameter\(p\text{.}\) P_J of course is a function of the data and parameter \(p\text{.}\) But since our interest is to find \(p\) that maximizes this joint probability for the given data, we think of \(P_J\) as a function of \(p\text{.}\) In this context we call \(P_J\) by another name, likelihood function, often denoted by \(L\)
    \begin{equation} L(p) = P_J(p),\quad \text{ more generally},\ L(\theta) = P_J(\theta).\tag{1.11.8} \end{equation}
    Thus, in the present example, we have the following function that we need to maximize with respect to the paremeter \(p\) to obtain the maximum likelihood estimate of \(p\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} L(p) = p^{\sum_i x_i} (1-p)^{\sum_i(1-x_i)}. \end{equation*}
    It will be less verbose to write \(\overline{x}\) for the sample mean of the data at hand. Let
    \begin{equation*} \overline{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \end{equation*}
    Then, likelihood for the \(n\) Bernoulli experiments is
    \begin{equation*} L(p) = p^{n\overline{x}} (1-p)^{ n - n\overline{x}}. \end{equation*}
    Notice that taking log both sides will turn this product into a sum. And, since log is a monotonic function of its argument, the maximum of a log of a function will occur at the same place as the function itself.
    \begin{equation*} \log_{\text{max }p}\left( L(p) \right) = L_{\text{max }p}(p). \end{equation*}
    For brevity, let us denote the log of likelihood by \(LL\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} LL (p) \equiv \log ( L (p) ) = \left(n\overline{x} \right) \log p + \left( n - n\overline{x} \right) \log (1-p). \end{equation*}
    Taking derivative with respect to \(p\) and setting \(p=p_\text{MLE}\text{,}\) we can solve for \(p_\text{MLE}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d LL(p)}{dp} = \frac{n\overline{x}}{p_\text{MLE}} - \frac{n - n\overline{x}}{1-p_\text{MLE}}. \end{equation*}
    Noting that \(\sum_i 1 = n\) here, we get
    \begin{equation} p_\text{MLE} = \overline{x}.\tag{1.11.9} \end{equation}
    That is, the MLE estimate of the proportion \(p\) just the average of the collected data on heads and tails, with heads being \(1\) and tails being \(0\text{.}\)
    Theorem 1.11.1.
    MLE estimate is maximum of the log likelihood function.
    Proof.
    To show that the extremeum found is the maximum and not the minimum, all we have to do is to check whether the sign of the second derivative when \(p=p_\text{MLE}\) negative or not.
    \begin{align*} \left. \frac{d^2LL}{dp^2} \right|_{p = p_\text{MLE}} \amp = -\frac{n\overline{x}}{p_\text{MLE}^2} + \frac{n -n\overline{x} }{ \left( 1 - p_\text{MLE} \right)^2 }\\ \amp = -\frac{n\overline{x}}{\overline{x}^2} + \frac{n ( 1 -\overline{x}) }{ \left( 1 - \overline{x} \right)^2 }\\ \amp = -\frac{n}{\overline{x}} + \frac{n }{ \left( 1 - \overline{x} \right) } \\ \amp = \frac{ -1}{ \overline{x} (\left( 1 - \overline{x} \right)) } \lt 0. \end{align*}
    Hence, MLE estimate is indeed a maximum of Likelihood function.\(\quad\blacksquare\)
    Remark 1.11.2. What if \(X\) was not Bernoulli?
    We just have to proceed a little differently after Eq. (1.11.7). In that equation, parameters, usually denoted by \(\theta\text{,}\) of interest will appear in the probability distributions of the individual trials, same for every one of them.
    \begin{equation} P_J(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n) = \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i;\theta).\tag{1.11.10} \end{equation}
    Then, the likelihood function will just be
    \begin{equation} L(\theta) = \Pi_{i=1}^n P_X(x_i;\theta).\tag{1.11.11} \end{equation}
    The loglikelihood
    \begin{equation} LL(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \left( P_X(x_i;\theta)\right).\tag{1.11.12} \end{equation}
    In general, setting the derivative of \(LL\) to zero and solving for the MLE estimate can only be done numercally.
    Example 1.11.3. Each Trial \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) of unknown \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\).
    We have
    \begin{equation*} P_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}\, e^{ -(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2 }}. \end{equation*}
    In this case, suppose we want an MLE of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\text{.}\) The log likelihood function will be (replacing \(\theta\) by the actual parameters here in Eq. (1.11.12).)
    \begin{align*} LL(\mu, \sigma^2) \amp \sum_{i=1}^n \log \left( P_X(x_i;\theta)\right). \\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^n\left[ - \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi \sigma^2) - \frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right]. \end{align*}
    Now, to find \(\mu_\text{MLE}\) and \(\sigma_\text{MLE}\) you will solve the following equations.
    \begin{align*} \amp \left. \frac{\partial LL(\mu, \sigma^2)}{\partial \mu} \right|_{\mu_\text{MLE}, \sigma^2_\text{MLE}} = 0 \\ \amp \left. \frac{\partial LL(\mu, \sigma)}{\partial \sigma^2} \right|_{\mu_\text{MLE}, \sigma^2_\text{MLE}} = 0 \end{align*}
    Show that you get the following answers.
    \begin{align*} \amp \mu_\text{MLE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n x_i \equiv \bar x,\\ \amp \sigma_\text{MLE}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n \left(x_i - \bar x \right)^2 \end{align*}
    The MLE estimate of the variance is a biased estimator. Often, we will use the following unbiased estimate for variance, also called the sample variance.
    \begin{equation*} s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i}^n \left(x_i - \bar x \right)^2. \end{equation*}
    Below we discuss the technical meaning of bias and unbias.
    Definition 1.11.4. Estimators.
    From the formulas for MLE etimates of \(p\) of a Bernoulli \(X\) and \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) of a Gaussian \(X\text{,}\) we can write random variables whose expectation values estimate these quantities. We call these random variables estimators. We will denote them with capital letters as we have done for other random variables.
    Thus the MLE estimator of \(p\) of a Bernoulli variable \(X\) will be
    \begin{equation} \Pi_\text{MLE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i, \text{ same as }\overline{X}.\tag{1.11.13} \end{equation}
    Similarly, the MLE estimators of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) for a Gaussian/Normal variable \(X\) will be
    \begin{align} \amp M_\text{MLE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i, \text{ same as }\overline{X},\tag{1.11.14}\\ \amp \Sigma_\text{MLE}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n \left(X_i - M \right)^2\tag{1.11.15} \end{align}
    If expectation values of these estimators in their corresponding probability distributions yield the appropriate true values, \(p\) for the Bernoulli case, and \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) for the Normal case, we say that the estimator is an unbiased estimartor.
    Bernoulli \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\) estimator is unbiased..
    To prove that \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\) is unbiased, we calculate it/s expectation value in the Bernoulli PMF. We know that for expectation value of each \(X_i\) is same:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X_i] = p. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\left[ \Pi_\text{MLE} \right] \amp = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}\left[ X_i \right] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} p = \frac{1}{n} \times n\,p = p. \end{align*}
    Hence \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\) is an unbiased estimator of \(p\text{.}\) \(\quad \blacksquare\)
    Gaussian \(M_\text{MLE}\) estimator is unbiased..
    Here, we know that \(\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu\) for each \(X_i\text{.}\) Hence, just like the Bernoulli \(\Pi_\text{MLE}\text{,}\) the expectation value of \(M_\text{MLE}\) is also unbiased.
    Gaussian \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\) estimator is not unbiased..
    Now, we come to an estimator which is not unbiased. Let’s see. Since each \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\text{,}\) the variance of each \(X_i\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] - \left( \mathbb{E}[X_i] \right)^2 = \sigma^2 \implies \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\tag{1.11.16} \end{equation}
    When we expand the parenthesis in Eq. (1.11.15), we will find that we also need:
    \begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[M^2] = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2\tag{1.11.17} \end{equation}
    Here is how I got this result.
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[M^2] \amp = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n X_i X_j\right]\, \quad\text{Use two indices, one for each }M.\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2}\mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=j} X_i X_j + \sum_{i\ne j} X_i X_j \right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\left[X_i^2\right] + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i\ne j} \mathbb{E}\left[X_i X_j\right] \\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 \right) + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i\ne j} \mu\times \mu,\ (\text{ since }X_i \text{ and } X_{j\ne i}\text{ independent.})\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n^2} \left(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 \right) \sum_{i=1}^n 1 + \frac{1}{n^2} \mu^2 \sum_{i\ne j} 1.\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n} \left(\sigma^2 + \mu^2 \right) + \frac{n(n-1)}{n^2} \mu^2,\quad \text{ since} \sum_{i\ne j} 1 = n(n-1). \\ \amp = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2. \end{align*}
    Another term that will show up in our calculation is
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[MX_i] = \sigma^2 + n\,\mu^2. \end{equation*}
    I leave this part to you to complete. Now, we look at the expectation of the estimator \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}\left[ \Sigma_\text{MLE}^2 \right] \amp = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^n \left(X_i - M \right)^2 \right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1^n} \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] - \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[MX_i] + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[M^2] \\ \amp = \frac{1}{n}\times n\, \left( \sigma^2 + \mu^2\right) - \frac{2}{n} \left( \sigma^2 + n\,\mu^2 \right) + \frac{1}{n}\times n \left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2 \right)\\ \amp = \frac{n-1}{n}\,\sigma^2 \ne \sigma^2 \end{align*}
    Hence \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\) is not an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\) of a Normal variable. \(\qquad \blacksquare\)
    Definition 1.11.5. Unbiased Sample Variance.
    From the result above, it is immediately obvious that had we divided by \((n-)\) in the definition of \(\Sigma_\text{MLE}^2\) rather than by \(n\text{,}\) the modified quantity, to be denoted by \(S^2\) will be an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( X_i - M \right)^2.\tag{1.11.18} \end{equation}
    We call this quantity Sample Variance. It is a random variable since it depends upon the random variables \(X_i\)’s and \(M\text{.}\)
    \(S^2\) is unbiased.
    You can verify that expectation value of sample variance will be the (unknown) population variance \(\sigma^2\) by a more direct calculation but a less algebraic way of proving the unbiasedness of \(S^2\) is to notice its relation to chi square disrtribution.
    Consider a Gaussian variable \(X\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and sample variance defined by \(S^2\) formula defined above. Then, following is true of a related variable \(Y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} Y \equiv \frac{(n-1) S^2 }{\sigma^2} \sim \chi_{n-1}^2. \end{equation*}
    Let’s use \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) symbol for \(Y\) to remind us of its distribution. And, rearrange the above relation to
    \begin{equation*} S^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n-1}\,\chi^2_{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    Clearly, the expectation value of \(S^2\) will be simply
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[S^2] = \frac{\sigma^2}{n-1}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\chi^2_{n-1}\right]. \end{equation*}
    But the expectation value of \(\chi^2\) variable is just its degree of freedom, which is \((n-1)\) here. Hence,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[S^2] = \frac{\sigma^2}{n-1}\,\mathbb{E}\times (n-1) = \sigma^2\quad \implies S^2\text{ unbiased}. \quad \blacksquare \end{equation*}
    Interestingly, although \(S^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\text{,}\) its square root \(\Sigma_\text{uMLE}\) is not an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma\text{.}\)

    Subsection 1.11.2 Confidence Interval

    From the point estimation section above, we learned how to use maximum likelihood procedure on the sampled data for computing parameters that model distribution of a random variable in nature. For instance, we saw that sample mean gives an unbiased estimate of the probability of success (\(1\)’s) in a \(0/1\) system. But, how can we be sure that the estimated value is close to the true value since the later is not known? This is where the idea of confidence interval comes in.
    A confidence interval for a parameter \(\theta\) is an interval of values, \((\theta_\text{low}, \theta_\text{high})\) which could contain the true unknown (maybe, even unknowable) value of \(\theta\) at a particular level of confidence.
    The confidence level is usually written as a percentage using a complementary quantity \(\alpha\) (alpha), which is written as a fraction of \(1\text{,}\) as follows.
    \begin{equation*} \text{Confidence Level } = (1-\alpha)100\%, \end{equation*}
    The fractional quantity \((1-\alpha)\) is called confidence coefficient. For instance,, if we want \(95\%\) confidence level, we would have
    \begin{equation*} 95\%\,\text{Confidence Level} \iff \alpha = 1- 0.95 = 0.05. \end{equation*}
    Clearly, greater the confidence level (or, equivalently, smaller \(\alpha\)), greater should be our confidence that the unknown parameter is contained somewhere inside the confidence interval.

    Definition 1.11.6. Confidence Interval.

    A confidence interval \((\theta_\text{low}, \theta_\text{high})\) of an unknown parameter \(\theta\) of a statistical model is defined by the probability of the event that the interval contains the true value of the parameter be equal to the desired confidence level. That is,
    \begin{equation} P(\theta_\text{low} \lt \theta \lt \theta_\text{high}) = (1-\alpha).\tag{1.11.19} \end{equation}
    We will see examples of how we can compute \(\theta_\text{low}\) and \(\theta_\text{high}\) from the data collected.

    Subsubsection 1.11.2.1 The Z Intervals

    The confidence interval, called the Z interval, is a confidence interval of the population mean \(\mu\) when we are provided with the exact value of the population variance \(\sigma^2\text{.}\) At this point, let’s not worry about how we somehow know the population variance but not the population mean. We are assuming this so that we can apply the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) to this problem. The procedure is best illustrated by a numerical example.
    Supose our project is to study height of male adults in a populatoin in which the height varies with a known variance of \(\sigma^2 = 25\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\) We wish to find the confidence interval of the mean height \(\mu\) at the confidence level of \(95\%\text{,}\) i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} \alpha = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05. \end{equation*}
    That is, we want to find \(h_\text{low}\) and \(h_\text{high}\) so that the following statement is true.
    \begin{equation*} P( h_\text{low} \lt h \lt h_\text{high} ) = 0.95, \end{equation*}
    where \(h\) is the true mean of all adult males in the population, which could, in principle, be obtained by measuring heights of every adult male, but, in practice, unpractical.
    Notation: We use capital letters for the random variable name and small letters for their values. Thus, we will use \(H\) for the variable height, \(h_i\) for individual heights, \(h\) for population mean, \(\overline{H}\) for sample mean, \(\overline{h}\) for the value of the sample mean from the sample collected.
    Therefore, we collect a sample by measuring the heights of \(50\) randomly selected adults. From which, we can, of course, find the sample mean \(\overline{h}\text{.}\) Say, we found the result \(170 \, \text{cm}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \overline{h} \equiv \overline{H} = \frac{1}{50}\sum_{i=1}^{50}h_i = 170\, \text{cm}. \end{equation*}
    We are also given the population variance of heights to be:
    \begin{equation*} \sigma^2 = 25\,\text{cm}^2. \end{equation*}
    Our task is to find values of \(h_\text{low}\) and \(h_\text{high}\) from \(n\text{,}\) \(\overline{h}\text{,}\) and \(\sigma^2\text{.}\)
    Remark 1.11.7. The Answer.
    Let me just give you the answer first and then we will look at the theory based on our thinking that the distribution is more or less like a Gaussian distribution.
    1. Assuming, the included probability is the central part of a Gaussian, we can implement the probability required in Eq. (1.11.19) by using the standard normal distribution’s CDF, \(\Phi\text{.}\)
      We want to include \((1-\alpha)\) amount of central part of Gaussian and leave out equal amount, say \(x\) on either side, with total being \(1\text{.}\)
      \begin{equation*} x + (1-\alpha) + x = 1. \end{equation*}
      That gives \(x = \alpha/2\text{.}\) Hence, to get \(Z\) value at the upper end, we need to find \(z\) which gives probability \(1-\alpha/2\text{.}\) That is, we need to solve the following problems for \(z_\text{high}\text{:}\)
      \begin{align*} \amp \Phi(z_\text{high}) = 1 - \frac{\alpha}{2}. \end{align*}
      This will be
      \begin{equation*} z_\text{high} = \Phi^{-1} \left( 1 - \frac{\alpha}{2} \right) \end{equation*}
      For \(\Phi^{-1}(k) \text{,}\) you can use a numeerical tool such as scipy.stats.norm.ppf(k). When I looked up the number for \(k=0.95\text{,}\) the value was approximately 1.96.
      We call this value \(z_{\alpha/2}\text{.}\)
      \begin{equation} z_\text{high} \equiv z_{\alpha/2}.\tag{1.11.20} \end{equation}
      From symmetry of the standard normal about \(z=0\text{,}\) we arrive at
      \begin{equation} z_\text{low} = - z_\text{high} = - z_{\alpha/2}.\tag{1.11.21} \end{equation}
    2. Calculate the high and low limits of the confidence interval by
      \begin{align*} \amp h_\text{low} = \overline{h} - z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \\ \amp h_\text{high} = \overline{h} + z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \end{align*}
    So, that’s all there is to the \(Z\)-confidence interval.
    The Dtails:
    Recall that according to CLT, if a variable \(H\) has population mean \(\mu\text{,}\) population variance \(\sigma^2\text{,}\) and \(n\) points of data, \(H_1, H_2, \cdots, H_n\text{,}\) with sample mean \(\overline{H}\text{,}\) then, for large enough \(n\text{,}\) the following variable \(Z\) constructed from them has a standard normal distribution.
    \begin{equation} Z \equiv \frac{\overline{H} - \mu}{ \sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),\tag{1.11.22} \end{equation}
    Suppose, instead of Eq. (1.11.19), we start with first trying to find \(z_\text{low}\) and \(z_\text{high}\) with the same probability,
    \begin{equation} P\left( z_\text{low} \lt Z \lt z_\text{high} \right) = 1-\alpha = 0.95.\tag{1.11.23} \end{equation}
    Can we then somehow transform this to the Eq. (1.11.19)? Following steps show you how.
    \begin{align*} \amp P\left( z_\text{low} \lt \frac{\overline{H} - \mu}{ \sigma/\sqrt{n}} \lt z_\text{high} \right) = 1-\alpha\\ \amp P\left( z_\text{low} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \lt \overline{H} - \mu \lt z_\text{high} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha \\ \amp P\left( z_\text{high} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \gt \mu - \overline{H} \gt z_\text{low} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha\\ \amp P\left( \overline{H} + z_{high} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \gt \mu \gt \overline{H} + z_\text{low} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha\\ \amp P\left(\overline{H} + z_\text{low} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \lt \mu \lt \overline{H} + z_\text{high} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha \end{align*}
    This shows that the \(h_\text{low}\) and\(h_\text{high}\) of the confidence interval are
    \begin{align} \amp h_\text{low} = \overline{H} + z_\text{low} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{1.11.24}\\ \amp h_\text{high} = \overline{H} + z_\text{high} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{1.11.25} \end{align}
    How do you get these \(z_\text{low}\) and \(z_\text{high}\text{?}\) To find these values, we look at the PDF and CDF of a standard Normal disribution as shown in Figure 1.11.8. The CDF shows that to include the middle \((1-\alpha)\) of the probability, we need the following values of \(Z\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} z_\text{low} = - z_{\alpha/2}, \quad z_\text{high} = z_{\alpha/2}. \end{equation*}
    The value of \(z_{\alpha/2}\) corresponds to the probability value
    \begin{equation*} p_{\alpha/2} = 1-\frac{\alpha}{2} = 1 - \frac{0.05}{2} = 0.975. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, we solve the following equation to get \(z_{high}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} \Phi(z_\text{high}) = p_{\alpha/2}.\tag{1.11.26} \end{equation}
    Inverting this equation gives
    \begin{equation} z_\text{high} \equiv z_{\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}( p_{\alpha/2} ) = \Phi^{-1}( 0.975 ) \approx 1.96.\tag{1.11.27} \end{equation}
    This solved our immediate problem of \(z_\text{low}\) and \(z_\text{high}\text{.}\)
    Working out the numerical values in the example data we find the confidence interval.
    \begin{align*} \amp \overline{H} = 170\,\text{cm}\\ \amp z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 1.96\times \frac{5\,\text{cm}}{\sqrt{50}} = 1.39\,\text{ cm}\\ \amp \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} - 1.39\,\text{cm} = 168.61\,\text{cm}\\ \amp \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} + 1.39\,\text{cm} = 171.39\,\text{cm} \end{align*}
    Hence, the \(95\%\) confidence interval for heights of adult males in the population is \((168.61\,\text{cm},\ 171.39\,\text{cm})\text{.}\) This interval is called Z interval because we use the Standard Normal distribution. It is applicable when we use a known population variance. As has already been pointed out that this is not the case in real settings. The variance must also be estimated from the collected data. We turn to remedy this problem in the next section.
    Correct and Wrong Interpretations of Confidence Intervals: How should you interpret the following probability statement?
    \begin{equation*} P\left(\overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \lt \mu \lt \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1-\alpha \end{equation*}
    To simplify notation in our discussion, let’s rewrite this equation with \(L\) for the lower end and \(U\) for the upper end of the Z-interval.
    \begin{equation} P\left(L \lt \mu \lt U \right) = 1-\alpha,\tag{1.11.28} \end{equation}
    with
    \begin{equation*} L = \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\quad U = \overline{H}+ z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}. \end{equation*}
    Notice that \(\overline{H}\) depends on the data \(\{ h_1, h_2, \cdots, h_{50}\}\) we had collected by measuring the heights of \(50\) adult males chisen randomly from the population. Therefore, \(L\) and \(U\) depend on the data as well. It’s highly likely that if we repeat this experiment of randomly selecting \(50\) adult males and measuring their heights, you will get a different result. Hence, \(L\) and \(U\) are also random variables.
    But population mean \(\mu\) is one definitely number, which would the average of ALL males in the population. Although \(\mu\) is not known but there is no uncertainty in what it is supposed to be. Therefore, we cannot say probability of \(\mu\) to be in this interval is such and such - you can only assign probability to uncertain events and value of \(\mu\) is not uncertain.
    Here, the uncertainty is in the confidence interval since both \(L\) and \(U\) are uncertain, depending on the data instances. As you repeat collection of data, you will get different \((L,U)\) from each data instance. Some of those ranges will include \(\mu\) some will not. The way to interpret Eq. (1.11.28) is that among numerous repeats of data, \((-1\alpha)100\%\) of the time the \((L,U)\) range will include \(\mu\text{.}\) Whether a particular interval will include the \(\mu\) is either a yes or no answer, not a probability answer. You might ask about the probability of the event that any one of the confidence interval includes \(\mu\) in it - the answer to that probability will be \((1-\alpha)100\%\text{.}\)

    Subsubsection 1.11.2.2 The t-Intervals

    Let’s study the same problem of determining the mean height of a population of male adults in a population. We had collected \(50\) heights, \(\{h_1, h_2, \cdots, h_{50} \}\) and computed their sample mean to be \(\overline{h} = 170 \, \text{cm}\text{.}\) We know nothing about either population mean \(\mu\) or population variance \(\sigma^2\text{.}\) But, in addition to \(\overline{h}\text{,}\) we can compute sample variance from the \(h_i\)’s, e.g., the unbiased sample mean.
    \begin{equation} S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n\left( H_i - \overline{H} \right),\tag{1.11.29} \end{equation}
    where I have written everything in terms of the randdom variables for each sampling step, viz., \(\left(H_1, H_2, \cdots, H_{n}\right)\) with \(n=50\) here, and
    \begin{equation*} \overline{H} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} H_i. \end{equation*}
    Suppose, we found that the value of \(S^2\) from our data, now denoted by the lower case letter, since we are talking about a value of \(S^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} s^2 = 49\, \text{cm}^2 \end{equation*}
    How would we now use \(\overline{h}\) and \(s^2\) to find confidence interval at confidence level \((1-\alpha)100\%\text{?}\)
    This is where the theory of Student \(t\) distribution comes handy since the following variable \(T\) obes a \(t\) distribution of degrees of freedom equal to \((n-1)\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} T = \frac{ \overline{H}- \mu }{ S/\sqrt{n} } \sim t_{n-1}.\tag{1.11.30} \end{equation}
    Proof.
    Note that \(T\) contstructed in Eq. (1.11.30) is different than the \(T\) we had defined when we discussed the \(t\)-distribution. There, we had the degree of freedom of the \(t\)-distribution in the definition. Let \(r\) be the degree of freedom, in place of \(n\) used nefore so that we separate the \(n\) from samples taken to \(r\) for the degree of freedom.
    \begin{equation*} T_\text{before} = \frac{Z}{Y/\sqrt{r}} \sim t_{r}, \end{equation*}
    where it was assumed that
    \begin{equation*} Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\qquad Y\sim \chi^2_{r}. \end{equation*}
    In Eq. (1.11.30) we need to deal with \(S^2\text{,}\) for which we will make use of the result:
    \begin{equation*} (n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}.\qquad \text{(We will prove this later.)} \end{equation*}
    Let’s now make the following manipulations of the expression in Eq. (1.11.30).
    \begin{align*} T \amp = \frac{ \overline{H}- \mu }{ S/\sqrt{n} } = \frac{ \frac{ \overline{H}- \mu }{\sigma\sqrt{n}} }{ S/\sigma } = \frac{ Z }{ S/\sigma } = \frac{ Z }{ \sqrt{ S^2/\sigma^2} } = \frac{ Z }{ \sqrt{ Y/r} } \end{align*}
    Here, \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and
    \begin{equation*} Y = r\, S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\, \text{ if } r = n-1. \end{equation*}
    Hence, we have the conditions for \(t_{n-1}\) distribution for the defined \(T\) variable:
    \begin{equation*} T = \frac{Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)}{ \sqrt{(Y \sim \chi^2_{n-1})/(n-1)} }, \end{equation*}
    which is \(t\)-distribution of degree of freedom equal \((n-1)\text{.}\) \(\qquad \blacksquare\)
    Figure 1.11.9 shows parallels to Figure 1.11.8 and illustrates that all we need to do is to use \(t\) distribution of \((n-1) = 50-1 = 49\) degrees of freedom here. The parallels of \(T\) here and \(Z\) in Eq. (1.11.22) is obvious with only changes being
    \begin{equation*} \sigma \longrightarrow S, \quad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \longrightarrow T\sim t_{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    The value of \(T\) separating the included exluded excluded probabilities is very close to the corresponding \(Z\) values since we have a fairly high degree of freedom.
    \begin{equation*} t_{n-1, \alpha/2} = t_{50-1, 0.025} \approx 2.01 \text{ compare to } z_{0.025} \approx 1.96. \end{equation*}
    The difference is atrtributable to the flat tail aspect of the \(t\) distribution compared to the standard normal distribution.
    Therefore, the \(t\) confidence interval at confidence interval \((1-\alpha)100%\) is
    \begin{equation} \left(\overline{H}- t_{n-1, \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\ \overline{H} + t_{n-1, \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right) \tag{1.11.31} \end{equation}
    Numerically, in our example, we will get
    \begin{align*} \amp \overline{H} = 170\,\text{cm}\\ \amp t_{n-1, \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = 2.01\times \frac{7\,\text{cm}}{\sqrt{50}} = 1.99\,\text{ cm}\\ \amp \overline{H}- z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} - 1.99\,\text{cm} = 168.01\,\text{cm}\\ \amp \overline{H} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 170\,\text{cm} + 1.99\,\text{cm} = 171.99\,\text{cm} \end{align*}
    That is the \(t\) distribution will be
    \begin{equation*} t\text{-Confidence Interval at } 95\% = \left( 168.01\,\text{cm},\ 171.99\,\text{cm} \right). \end{equation*}

    Subsection 1.11.3 Difference Between Two Means

    Often we are faced with trying to figure out if the means of two different populations are reasonably same, without having any clue about their actual values. Above we learned what to do with saying something definite about the mean of one population from a sample of the data. So, here we will want to say something about their difference.
    For a simpler setting, as we did with the \(Z\) intervals, we could assume that we somehow know the population variances and further that they are equal. That appears to be too drastic an assumption. Instead, we will say, we want to get all the information from the data collected from the two populations. Except that we will assume the distributions are Gaussian/Normal.
    Let give names \(X\) and \(Y\) to the variables in the two populations. Let their means be denoted by \(\mu_X\) and \(\mu_Y\text{,}\) respectively, and similarly for their unknown variances.
    \begin{equation*} X\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2),\quad Y\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2). \end{equation*}
    We wish to detemine \(t\)-Confidence Interval of \(\mu_X - \mu_Y\) at the confidence level \((1-\alpha)\text{.}\)
    Let data collected from the two populations be
    \begin{align*} \mathcal{D}_X\amp = \{X_1, X_2, \cdots, X_n \} \\ \mathcal{D}_Y\amp = \{Y_1, Y_2, \cdots, Y_m \} \end{align*}
    From these data we can evaluate their sample means and sample variances.
    \begin{align*} \amp \overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\\ \amp \overline{Y} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m Y_i\\ \amp S_X^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( X_i - \overline{X} \right)^2\\ \amp S_Y^2 = \frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^m \left( Y_i - \overline{Y} \right)^2 \end{align*}

    Subsubsection 1.11.3.1 Equal Variance Populations: Pooled t-test

    Suppose we suspect that \(\sigma^2\) for the two populations are identical, then we would pool the data and compute a single pooled “best” sample variance.
    \begin{equation} S_p^2 = \frac{ (n-1)S_X^2 + (m-1)S_Y^2 }{n+m-2}.\tag{1.11.32} \end{equation}
    Then the confidence interval on \(\mu_X - \mu_Y\) can be deduced from the following theorem.
    Theorem 1.11.10.
    Let two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) be normal distributions with equal variances.
    \begin{equation*} X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma^2),\quad Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma^2). \end{equation*}
    From the data \(\mathcal{D}_X\) and \(\mathcal{D}_Y\text{,}\) we compute the their sample means \(\overline{X}\text{,}\) \(\overline{Y}\) and pooled variance,\(S_p\text{.}\) Then, the following variable has a \(t\)-distribution.
    \begin{equation*} T = \frac{ (\overline{X} - \overline{Y}) - (\mu_X - \mu_Y) }{ S_p \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{m} \right)} \sim t_{n+m-2}. \end{equation*}
    We are not going to prove this theorem. From this \(t\)-distribution, however, you can discover the \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval of the population mean difference will be
    \begin{equation*} \left(\overline{x} - \overline{y}\right) \pm t_{n+m-2, \alpha/2}\,s_p \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{m} \right) \end{equation*}
    where all the lower case letters for variables indicate the values obtained on the corresponding datasets.

    Subsubsection 1.11.3.2 Welch t test

    The above described pooled variance works when the variances of the two populations being compared are equal. When we cannot make that assumption, we turn to the Welch’s \(t\)-test for guidance in setting confidence interval for the difference of means.
    Let’s start with the answer first. Welch showed that upto two moments of the distribution, the following variable has a \(t\)-distribution of degree of freedom \(r\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} T = \frac{Z_w}{ \sqrt{ \frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} } } \sim t_r, \end{equation*}
    where
    \begin{align*} \amp Z_w = \frac{ (\overline{X} - \overline{Y}) - (\mu_X - \mu_Y) }{ \sqrt{ \frac{s_X^2}{n} + \frac{s_Y^2}{m} } },\\ \amp r = \frac{ \left( \frac{s_X^2}{n} + \frac{s_Y^2}{m} \right)^2 }{ \frac{s_X^4}{n^2(n-1)} + \frac{s_Y^4}{m^2(m-1)} }, \end{align*}
    where \(s_x\) and \(s_Y\) are sample standard deviation. Using this result, it is straight forward procedure, as shown for single mean case to find the \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval for the difference in means of two separate populations.
    \begin{equation} \overline{X} - \overline{Y} \pm t_{r,\alpha/2}\, \sqrt{ \frac{s_X^2}{n} + \frac{s_Y^2}{m} } \tag{1.11.33} \end{equation}
    The example at the end of this section illustrates a use of this formula.
    The Hard Work:.
    To start with consider the difference in the sample means:
    \begin{equation*} W = \overline{X} - \overline{Y}. \end{equation*}
    The expectation and variance of \(W\) is readily calculated from the expectation and variance of means.
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbb{E}[W] = \mu_X - \mu_Y,\quad \text{ and },\\ \amp \textrm{Var}(W) = \textrm{Var}(\overline{X}) + \textrm{Var}(\overline{X}) = \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma_Y^2}{m}. \end{align*}
    Therefore, we can construct a normal variable by subtracting the expectation value from \(W\) and scaling by the square root of its variance. Therefore, the variable \(Z_w\) defined by
    \begin{equation*} Z_w = \frac{ W - (\mu_X - \mu_Y) }{ \sqrt{ \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma_Y^2}{m} } } \end{equation*}
    should have the standard normal distribution.
    \begin{equation*} Z_w \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1). \end{equation*}
    We can’t use \(Z_w\) to construct a confidence interval that depends solely on the data collected since it has the unknown population variances \(\sigma_X^2\) and \(\sigma_Y^2\text{.}\) What would happen if we replace them with sample variances? That is, we want to look at the following as a candidate, similar to the \(t\)-confidence interval for mean from the \(Z\)-confidence interval we saw above:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{ W - (\mu_X - \mu_Y) }{ \sqrt{ \frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} } } \end{equation*}
    We want to convert this into a \(t\) variable. First let us convert the numerator to \(Z_w\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{ Z_w \, \sqrt{ \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma_Y^2}{m} } }{ \sqrt{ \frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} } }. \end{equation*}
    If this quantity could be transformed into the following form
    \begin{equation*} \frac{Z}{ \sqrt{Y/r} },\text{ with } Z\sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),\ \ Y\large{\sim} \chi^2_r, \end{equation*}
    for some \(r\text{,}\) this compisite quantity will go as \(t_{r}\)-distribution. But, it is not possible to do that. So, Welch looked at a closely related quantity that can be turned into a \(T\)-variable up to two moments of the distribution, i.e, only the mean and the variance. The quantity he constructed is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{ Z_w }{ \sqrt{ \frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} } }. \end{equation*}
    Now, we demand that this be equal to a multiplier \(c\) of a \(\chi_r^2\) variable and try to solve for \(r\) for equating mean and variances of the two sides.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} = c\, \chi_r^2. \end{equation*}
    Taking expectation and variance of the two sides gives:
    \begin{align} \amp\mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} \right] = \mathbb{E}\left[ c\, \chi^2_r \right],\tag{1.11.34}\\ \amp \textrm{Var}\left( \frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} \right) = \textrm{Var}\left( c\, \chi^2_r \right),\tag{1.11.35} \end{align}
    Now, we can use the following to simplify.
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbb{E}[c\,\chi^2_r] = c\, \mathbb{E}[\chi^2_r] = c\, r \\ \amp \textrm{Var}\left( c\, \chi^2_r \right) = c^2\,\textrm{Var}\left( \chi^2_r \right) = c^2\times 2 r\\ \amp \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{S_x^2}{n} \right] = \frac{1}{n} \mathbb{E}[S_x^2] = \frac{1}{n}\,s_X^2\\ \amp \textrm{Var}\left( \frac{S_X^2}{n} \right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \textrm{Var}( S_X^2) = \frac{1}{n^2}\frac{ 2 \sigma_X^4 }{n-1} \approx \frac{1}{n^2}\frac{ 2 s_X^4 }{n-1} \end{align*}
    The last one is obtained from taking variance of the following and repacing \(\sigma^2\) by the value fo sample variance from the collected data.
    \begin{equation*} (n-1)\,\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    Now, we can rewrite Eqs. (1.11.34) and (1.11.35) and solve for \(r\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} r = \frac{ \left( \frac{s_X^2}{n} + \frac{s_Y^2}{m} \right)^2 }{ \frac{s_X^4}{n^2(n-1)} + \frac{s_Y^4}{m^2(m-1)} }.\tag{1.11.36} \end{equation}
    This equation is called the Welch Satterthwaite Equation. Now, we all the ingredients to construct a \(t\)-distribution variable which we can use to find the required confidence interval.
    \begin{equation} T = \frac{Z_w}{\sqrt{\frac{S_X^2}{n} + \frac{S_Y^2}{m} }} \Large{\sim} \chi^2_r.\tag{1.11.37} \end{equation}
    After all this work, the Welch’s \(T\) leads to the following confidence interval at confidence level \((1-\alpha)100\%\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation} \overline{X} - \overline{Y} \pm t_{r,\alpha/2}\, \sqrt{ \frac{s_X^2}{n} + \frac{s_Y^2}{m} }. \tag{1.11.38} \end{equation}
    Example 1.11.11.
    Suppose we wish to compare the population mean heights of adult males from two separate populations. We measure heights of \(50\) adults from \(X\)-population and \(40\) from the \(Y\)-population. From the sample we calculate sample means and variance and find the following values.
    \begin{equation*} \overline{X} = 170\,\text{cm},\ s_X^2 = 49\,\text{cm}^2,\quad \overline{Y} = 165\,\text{cm},\ s_Y^2 = 64\,\text{cm}^2. \end{equation*}
    Find the confidence interval for the difference of their means \(\mu_X - \mu_Y\) at \(95\%\) confidence level.
    We first calculate the degree of freedom \(r\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} r = \frac{ \left( \frac{49}{50} + \frac{64}{40} \right)^2 }{ \frac{(49/50)^2}{49} + \frac{(64/40)^2}{39} } = 78.08 \end{equation*}
    For the confidence interval we need \(t_{r,\alpha/2}\text{.}\) for that we look up python scipy.stats.t.ppp(78.08, 0.025) for \(\alpha=0.05\text{,}\) i.e., \(95\%\) confidence level. We get a value of \(1.99\text{.}\) Therefore, the confidence interval is
    \begin{equation*} 170\,\text{cm} - 165\,\text{cm} \pm 1.99\, \sqrt{ \frac{49\,\text{cm}^2}{50} + \frac{64\,\text{cm}^2}{40} } = 5\,\text{cm} \pm 3.2\,\text{cm}. \end{equation*}
    That is \((1.8\,\text{cm}, 8.2\,\text{cm} ) \text{.}\) This says that zero is not in this range. So, if the claim was that the two populations have the same population means, then we could reject that claim at this level of confidence. These decisions are formalized more in the hypothesis testing section.

    Subsection 1.11.4 Confidence Interval of Variance

    If you were comparing two normal variables, you would naturally want to compare not only their means but also their variances to conclude if their distributions were more or less same or not. It turns out putting confidence interval on variance is readily done based on the fact that the following scaled sample variance of \(n\) samples \(\[X_i,\ i=1,2,\cdots, X_n\]\) from a Gaussian distribution of population variance \(\sigma^2\) varies as \(\chi^2_{n-1}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} (n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2} \large{\sim} \chi^2_{n-1},\tag{1.11.39} \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation*} S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\left( X_i - \overline{X} \right)^2,\ \ \overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i. \end{equation*}
    We have used this result before in the context of comapring two means. I present a proof of this claim below, just for completeness sake. Its okay if you want to skip over the proof. I will work out the formula for the confidence interval after the proof.
    So, how do we use Eq. (1.11.39) to obtain a \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval on \(\sigma^2\text{?}\) It’s the same routine. We atart with the probability statement and then mainipulate the event to write it in a way so that we can read off the confidence interval.
    I will flip the script a bit here, just for fun. We want \((L, U)\) so that
    \begin{align*} \amp P( L \lt \sigma^2 \lt U ) = 1- \alpha\\ \amp P\left( \frac{1}{L} \gt \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \gt \frac{1}{U} \right) = 1- \alpha \\ \amp P\left( \frac{(n-1)S^2}{L} \gt \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \gt \frac{(n-1)S^2}{U} \right) = 1- \alpha \\ \amp P\left( \frac{(n-1)S^2}{U} \lt \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \lt \frac{(n-1)S^2}{L} \right) = 1- \alpha \\ \amp P\left( a \lt \chi^2_{n-1}\lt b\right) = 1- \alpha \end{align*}
    In the last step, we replaced the composite quantity by a \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) variable to make connection with the CDF and inverse CDF of \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) distribution. The quantities \(a\) and \(b\) can be read off from a plot of the appropriate probability density, here \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) as shown in Figure 1.11.12, which are labeled in the figure as \(\chi^2_{n-1,\alpha/2}\) and \(\chi^2_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}\text{,}\) respectively. Therefore, substituting the value of \(S^2\) from the data, writing it as \(s^2\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \frac{(n-1)s^2}{U} = a = \chi^2_{n-1,\alpha/2},\quad \frac{(n-1)s^2}{L} = \chi^2_{n-1,1-\alpha/2} \end{equation*}
    This gives the desired upper and lower ends of the confidence interval for the population variance \(\sigma^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} \left( \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}},\quad \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{n-1,\alpha/2}} \right).\tag{1.11.40} \end{equation}

    Example 1.11.13. Numerical Example (Continued).

    In our heights example, let’s look at the first population, where we had sampled \(n=50\) adult males with sample mean height equal to \(\overline{x} = 170,\text{cm}\) and sample variance equal to \(s^2 = 49\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\) We want to get \((1-\alpha)100\% = 95\%\) confidence interval for the population variance \(\sigma^2\text{.}\) First we use scipy.stats.chi2.ppf(x, df) with \(x = \alpha/2\) and \(1-\alpha/2\) for \(df = n-1 = 49\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp \chi^2_{n-1,1-\alpha/2} = \text{scipy.stats.chi2.ppf}(0.975, 49) = 70.22.\\ \amp \chi^2_{n-1,\alpha/2} = \text{scipy.stats.chi2.ppf}(0.025, 49) = 31.55. \end{align*}
    These give the lower and upper values for the \(95\%\) confidence interval to be \((L,\ U)\) with
    \begin{align*} \amp L = \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}} = \frac{ (50-1) \times 49 }{70.22} = 34.19,\\ \amp U = \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{n-1, \alpha/2}} = \frac{ (50-1) \times 49 }{31.55} = 76.10. \end{align*}

    Proof of Eq. (1.11.39)..

    Let’s recall that if we have \(n\) standard normal variables \(Z_1, Z_2, \cdots, Z_n\) then sum of their squares has a \(\chi^2_n\) distribution.
    \begin{equation*} Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \cdots + Z_n^2 \large{\sim} \chi^2_n. \end{equation*}
    This was shown in detail when we discussed the \(\chi^2\) distribution.
    For our proof of Eq. (1.11.39), we will start with independent and identically disrtibuted (i.i.d.) \(n\) Gaussian variables \(X_i\)’s that are \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\text{.}\) Let us construct normal variables from them and sum their squares. Let
    \begin{equation*} W = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{X_i -\mu}{\sigma} \right)^2. \end{equation*}
    We want to introduce sample mean \(\overline{X}\) so that we can build the samole variance on the right side. We can do this by adding and subtracting \(\overline{X}\) in the numerator and expanding.
    \begin{align*} W\amp = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{(X_i - \overline{X}) +(\overline{X} -\mu)}{\sigma} \right)^2\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{ ( X_i - \overline{X} )^2 }{\sigma^2} + 2 \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{X_i - \overline{X}}{\sigma}\right)\ \left(\frac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma}\right) + \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2\\ \amp = (n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2} + 0 + n\, \left(\frac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2 \end{align*}
    The middle term became zero because \(\sum_i(X_i - \overline{X} ) = n\overline{X} - n \overline{X} = 0\text{.}\) The last term is actually a standard normal variable since the sample mean goes as
    \begin{equation*} \overline{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n ), \end{equation*}
    and we have subtracted the mean of this sample mean and scaled it by its standard deviation. Let’s rename it \(Z\text{.}\) Then, we have the following equation
    \begin{equation*} W = (n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2} + Z^2,\, \text{ where } W\sim \chi^2_n,\ Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). \end{equation*}
    For brevity in calculations below let us rename the first term on the right side \(R\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation} W = R + Z^2, \text{ or } R = W - Z^2.\tag{1.11.41} \end{equation}
    How can we get distributon of \(R\) if we know the distributions of \(W\) and \(Z\text{?}\) Since the rrelations are additive, moment generative function helps here. Moment generating function is defined by expectation value of an exponential function. For example, for \(W\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{M}_W(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tW}] \end{equation*}
    When you work this out for \(\chi^2_n\) distribution, yiu get
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[e^{tW}] = \frac{1}{(1-2t)^{n/2}} = (1-2t)^{-n/2}. \end{equation*}
    The \(Z^2\) variable can be thought of \(\chi^2_{n=1}\text{,}\) i.e., of a sum of standard normal with only one term. Hence, its’ moment generating function is
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{M}_{Z^2}(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tZ^2}] = \frac{1}{(1-2t)^{1/2}}. \end{equation*}
    Now, we go back to Eq. (1.11.41) to determine the moment generating function of \(R\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \end{equation*}
    \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[e^{tR}]\amp = \mathbb{E}[e^{t(W-Z^2)}] = \mathbb{E}[e^{tW}]\mathbb{E}[e^{-tZ^2}]= \frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{tW}]}{ \mathbb{E}[e^{tZ^2}] }\\ \amp = \frac{(1-2t)^{-n/2}}{(1-2t)^{-1/2}} = (1-2t)^{-(n-1)/2} \end{align*}
    This is just the moment generating function of a \(\chi^2_{n-1}\text{.}\) \(\quad\blacksquare\)

    Subsection 1.11.5 Comparing Variances of Two Normal Distributions

    Ability to compare variances can help us decide how similar or different two normal distributions are. Suppose we have two popolations \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2)\) and \(Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2)\text{.}\) Suppose we have collected \(n\) samples from \(X\) and \(m\) samples from \(Y\) from which we have computed the sample means and sample variances:
    \begin{align*} \amp\overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\\ \amp\overline{Y} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\\ \amp S_X^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\left( X_i - \overline{X} \right)^2\\ \amp S_Y^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^n\left( Y_i - \overline{Y} \right)^2 \end{align*}
    We have already tackled the question of comparing means \(\mu_X\) and \(\my_Y\) by finding the confidence interval in \(\mu_X - \mu_Y\text{.}\) Now, we wish to find someway to compare \(\sigma_X^2\) to \(\sigma_Y^2\text{.}\) It turns out that we can find the \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval on their ratio.
    \begin{equation} \frac{S_Y^2}{S_X^2}\,a \lt \frac{\sigma_X^2}{\sigma_Y^2} \lt \frac{S_Y^2}{S_X^2}\,b,\tag{1.11.42} \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation*} a = F_{n-1, m-1}^{-1}( \alpha/2 ),\quad a = F_{n-1, m-1}^{-1}( 1 - \alpha/2 ), \end{equation*}
    where \(F^{-1}\) is the inverse CDF of the \(F\)-distribution and the the subscripts in its symbol list the numerator and denominator dimensions of the \(F\)-distribution. We will derive the \(F\)-distribution PDF below. But here, we present how you would use them in calculating the confidence interval.

    Example 1.11.14. Numerical Example.

    Let’s go back to the heights of two population we have studied before. We had 50 samples from population \(X\) with sample mean equal to \(170\,\text{cm}\) and sample variance \(s_X^2 = 49\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\) We had \(40\) samples from population \(Y\) with sample mean equal to \(165\,\text{cm}\) and sample variance \(s_Y^2 = 64\,\text{cm}^2\text{.}\) What will be the \(95\%\text{,}\) i.e., \(\alpha=0.05\text{,}\) confidence interval of \(\sigma_Y^2/\sigma_X^2\text{?}\)
    Figure 1.11.15 shows the included and excluded area and the values of the right and left edges of the excluded probabilities.
    \begin{equation*} a = 0.54,\quad b = 1.81. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the lower and upper ends of the \(95\%\) confidence interval is
    \begin{align*} \amp L = \frac{s_Y^2}{s_X^2}\,a = \frac{64}{49}\times 0.54 = 0.705\\ \amp U = \frac{s_Y^2}{s_X^2}\,b = \frac{64}{49}\times 1.81 = 2.36 \end{align*}
    Interesting the value \(1.0\) is in the range \((0.705,\ 2.36)\) found here.

    Remark 1.11.16. How did the formula (1.11.42) come about?

    We will need to convert \(S_X^2\) and \(S_Y^2\) variables into \(\chi^2\) distributions. Let
    \begin{equation} A = (n-1)\frac{S_X^2}{\sigma_X^2},\quad A = (m-1)\frac{S_Y^2}{\sigma_Y^2}.\tag{1.11.43} \end{equation}
    From our previous discussions, you know that
    \begin{equation*} A \sim \chi^2_{n-1},\quad B\sim \chi^2_{m-1}. \end{equation*}
    For brevity of writing, let us introduce the degrees of freedoms by
    \begin{equation} u = n-1,\quad v = m-1.\tag{1.11.44} \end{equation}
    From \(A\) and \(B\) and their degrees of freedoms, let us construct a new variable, \(F\)
    \begin{equation} F = \frac{ A / u }{ B / v}.\tag{1.11.45} \end{equation}
    Then, as we will see below that \(F\) has the following ugly-looking PDF:
    \begin{equation} \rho_F(f;u,v) = \frac{\Gamma\left( \frac{u + v}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left( \frac{u}{2}\right) \Gamma\left( \frac{v}{2}\right)}\, \left( \frac{u}{v}\right)^{u/2}\ f^{v/2 -1}\,\left( 1 + \frac{u}{v}\,f \right)^{ -(u + v)/2 }.\tag{1.11.46} \end{equation}
    The \(A\) is called the numerator variable and \(B\) the denominator variable and \(u\) and \(v\) the numerator and denominator degrees of freedoms. This formula has already been programmed into stat packages. We just need to understand how to use it to get \((1-\alpha)100\%\) confidence interval. By now, I expect you to know the drill that you just look us the \(\textrm{scipy.stats.f.ppf()}\) function, which takes in three parameters \(\textrm{ppf(q, dfn=u, dfd=v)}\text{.}\) Thus, we require the following.
    \begin{equation} P( a \lt F \lt b ) = 1-\alpha.\tag{1.11.47} \end{equation}
    From the definition of CDF, \(F\text{,}\) we can say that
    \begin{equation} a = F^{-1}_{u,v}(\alpha/2), \quad b = F^{-1}_{u,v}(1 - \alpha/2).\tag{1.11.48} \end{equation}
    Now, we brign in the values of \(f\) in terms of \(A\) and \(B\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp P\left( a \lt \frac{ \frac{S_X^2}{\sigma_X^2}}{ \frac{S_Y^2}{\sigma_Y^2}} \lt b \right) = 1-\alpha. \\ \amp P\left( a\, \frac{S_Y^2}{S_X^2} \lt \frac{\sigma_Y^2}{\sigma_X^2} \lt b \, \frac{S_Y^2}{S_X^2} \right) = 1-\alpha. \end{align*}
    This shows the confidence interval of \(\sigma_Y^2/\sigma_X^2 \text{,}\) both unknwon quantities.

    Derivation of PDF of the F-distribution.

    First let’s copy the basic variables from definitions.
    \begin{equation*} F = \frac{A/u}{B/v}, \end{equation*}
    where \(A\) and \(B\) are independent variables.
    \begin{equation*} A \sim \chi^2_u,\quad B\sim \chi^2_v. \end{equation*}
    Since they are assumed to be independent, their joint probability will factorize.
    \begin{equation*} \rho_{AB}(a,b) da db = \rho_{A}(a) da \times \rho_{A}(a) db. \end{equation*}
    In transforming from \((A,B)\) to \(F\text{,}\) we are seeking a probability along lines of \(F\) in the \((A,B)\)-plane. The trick is to introduce another variable \(W\) which is identical to \(B\) then go from two-variable world of \((A,B)\) to a two-variable world of \((F,W)\text{.}\) You can then integrate out the \(W\) to obtain the probability density of just \(F\text{.}\)
    The transformation between the two sets of variables are:
    \begin{align*} \amp F = \frac{A/u}{B/v},\quad W = B\\ \amp A = \frac{u}{v}F W,\quad B = W \end{align*}
    By equating probability in an infinitesimal intervals in the \((A,B)\) and \((F,W)\) planes, we get
    \begin{equation*} \rho_{FW}(f,w) df dw = \rho_{AB}(a,b) da db = \left[\rho_{A}(a) \rho_{B}(b)\right]_{f,w} |J| df dw, \end{equation*}
    where the subscript \(f,w\) is instruction to cast the quantities within the brackets into \(f,w\) variables using the transformations above and \(|J|\) is the Jacobian.
    \begin{equation*} |J| = \det\begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial a}{\partial f} \amp \frac{\partial a}{\partial w}\\ \frac{\partial b}{\partial f} \amp \frac{\partial b}{\partial w} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{u}{v}\,w. \end{equation*}
    Since, we already know that \(A\) and \(B\) are \(\chi^2_u\) and \(\chi^2_v\) distributions, we can immediately write down the joint PDF of \(FW\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \rho_{FW}(f,w) \amp = \left[\rho_{A}(a) \rho_{B}(b)\right]_{f,w}\, \frac{u}{v}\,w\\ \amp = \left[\chi^2_u(a) \chi^2_v(b)\right]_{f,w}\, \frac{u}{v}\,w\\ \amp = \chi^2_u(u f w/v) \chi^2_v(w)\, \frac{u}{v}\,w\\ \amp = \frac{ (ufw/v)^{u/2 -1 } e^{-ufw/2v} \, w^{v/2-1} e^{-w/2}\,(u/v) w }{ 2^{u/2}\Gamma(u/2)\, 2^{v/2}\Gamma(v/2) } \end{align*}
    Now, we separate out \(w\)-dependent parts so we can integrate over this auxiliary variable. We get
    \begin{equation*} \frac{ (u/v)^{u/2} f^{u/2 - 1} }{ 2^{ \frac{u+v}{2} }\Gamma(u/2)\Gamma(v/2) } w^{\frac{u+v}{2}-1} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{uf}{v} + 1)\,w}. \end{equation*}
    Integrating away \(w\) just requires the following integral.
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^\infty w^{\frac{u+v}{2}-1} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{uf}{v} + 1)\,w} dw. \end{equation*}
    Changing the variable to
    \begin{equation*} t = \frac{1}{2}(\frac{uf}{v} + 1)\,w, \end{equation*}
    this integral becomes
    \begin{equation*} \left( \frac{1}{ \frac{1}{2}(\frac{uf}{v} + 1) } \right)^{ \frac{u+v}{2}} \int_0^\infty t^{\frac{u+v}{2}-1} e^{-t} dt. \end{equation*}
    The integral part, now simply a \(\Gamma\) function.
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma\left(\frac{u+v}{2} \right) = \int_0^\infty t^{\frac{u+v}{2}-1} e^{-t} dt. \end{equation*}
    Now, you can put all the factors together, which results in the cancellation of \(2\)’s to obtain the PDF of the \(F\) variable.
    \begin{align*} \rho_F(f) \amp = \int_{0}^{\infty}\,\rho_{FW}(f,w)\,dw \\ \amp = \frac{ \Gamma\left( \frac{u+v}{2}\right) }{ \Gamma\left( \frac{u}{2}\right)\,\Gamma\left( \frac{v}{2}\right) } \left( \frac{u}{v} \right)^{\frac{u}{2} } f^{\frac{u}{2} - 1} \left( 1 + \frac{u}{v}f \right)^{ \frac{u+v}{2} } \end{align*}
    This is of course normalized to probability \(1\) as you can verify by doing the following integral.
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^\infty\, \rho_F(f)\,df = 1. \end{equation*}
